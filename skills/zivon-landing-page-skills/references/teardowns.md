# Teardowns

Nine public pages, what each one teaches, and what not to copy. These are
observations of **structure** made on 2026-11-16, not measured tests of those
pages' performance; a page's visuals change quarterly and its argument changes
rarely. Link to them; never reproduce their copy or layout.

## How to read a page

Run these five passes, in order. They take fifteen minutes and produce something
usable; a moodboard produces nothing.

1. **The 360 px pass.** Load it on a phone viewport. What survives above the
   fold? That is what the page actually considers essential.
2. **The JS-off pass.** What is left? That is the page's argument without its
   demo.
3. **The heading pass.** Read only the headings, top to bottom. Is there a
   sentence?
4. **The evidence pass.** For every claim, find the proof. Count the claims that
   have none.
5. **The swap pass.** Put a competitor's wordmark on it. Does it still work?

Write down the *mechanism* ("the hero carries its own proof in the second line"),
never the *vibe* ("clean and modern"). A vibe cannot be implemented.

## 1. Stripe — `https://stripe.com`

**Accessed:** 2026-11-16. **Category:** developer infrastructure.

**The move.** An eyebrow states a scale claim about the global economy, and the
headline and subhead immediately convert it into three concrete capabilities
spanning a range — a first transaction and a billionth. Below, the page is dense
with real product surfaces — code, dashboards, diagrams — rather than
illustrations of them.

**What to learn.** A headline can carry its own proof if the number is defined in
the same breath. And density is not the enemy: *undifferentiated* density is.
Stripe earns a long page because every screen contains something a reader can
verify.

**What not to copy.** The density, the dark aesthetic, the developer-tool
vocabulary, the enormous page length. Zivon's reader is a 15-year-old on a 2 GB
phone; Stripe's is an engineer on a laptop. The principle transfers, the execution
does not.

**Ask your page:** does my headline define its own terms, or does it leave the
reader to guess what "interactive" means?

## 2. Linear — `https://linear.app`

**Accessed:** 2026-11-16. **Category:** product-development software.

**The move.** One category sentence, repeated with intent, then a product
screenshot that is clearly the actual application — dense, small type, real
issue keys — with the product's own interface as the hero image rather than a
stylised abstraction.

**What to learn.** Restraint is a differentiator when the category is loud. A
single sentence, held, is stronger than four claims competing. And a real
interface screenshot at small type says "this is a real tool" more convincingly
than any render.

**What not to copy.** The dark palette, the near-monochrome confidence, the
category-invention framing, and the repeated headline (the fetched HTML repeats
the same sentence several times — decorative repetition is not a lesson).

**Ask your page:** if I removed every sentence except one, which one survives —
and is it the right one?

## 3. GOV.UK — `https://www.gov.uk`

**Accessed:** 2026-11-16. **Category:** public service.

**The move.** The homepage is a task index, not a pitch. Plain English is
mandatory, sentences are short, headings are front-loaded and descriptive, and
nothing is sold. The page's confidence comes from how little it asks of the
reader.

**What to learn.** The plain-language evidence GOV.UK publishes is the best
available argument for short sentences and everyday words: people read a fraction
of a page, they scan in an F-shape, and plain English is preferred even by
specialists `[E]`. A page can be authoritative without being ornate.

**What not to copy.** The institutional flatness, the absence of a voice, the
task-index structure. Zivon is a product with a maker and a point of view; GOV.UK
is a service with neither. Copy the clarity, not the neutrality.

**Ask your page:** which sentence would a government plain-English editor cut
first — and why is it still there?

## 4. Basecamp — `https://basecamp.com`

**Accessed:** 2026-11-16. **Category:** project management.

**The move.** An opinionated positioning claim — the product as the
straightforward, easiest-to-use option — and a live-ish social signal (a running
count of people using it right now). The voice is a person's, not a brand's, and
the page is willing to be disliked by people who want complexity.

**What to learn.** A point of view is a differentiator. That positioning is a
claim about a stance, and it filters the audience on purpose. Filtering is a
feature on a landing page: the wrong reader leaving fast is cheaper than the
wrong reader signing up.

**What not to copy.** The cookie banner as the first interaction; the "right now"
counter unless the number is real and live; the long-feature-list sections lower
down. Also note that a stance only works when the product actually holds it —
Basecamp's simplicity is real, not a slogan.

**Ask your page:** what does this page say that a competitor would be uncomfortable
saying?

## 5. Wikimedia Foundation donation — `https://donate.wikimedia.org`

**Accessed:** 2026-11-16. **Category:** nonprofit, single ask.

**The move.** One action, one page, no navigation competing with it. A short
statement of purpose, a concrete number (the ask and what it funds), and a form.
The page has no second job.

**What to learn.** The purest example of "one page, one action". Every element
that is not the ask is either proof, an objection-remover, or legally required.
It is also a reminder that the ask itself can be small and specific (a named
amount) rather than vague.

**What not to copy.** The urgency framing (it is honest for an annual campaign,
not for a product), the donation-form complexity, the institutional voice. And do
not copy the single-ask structure onto a page whose reader does not yet know what
the product is — the donation page works because the reader already knows
Wikipedia.

**Ask your page:** how many elements would survive if the page had exactly one
job and no navigation?

## 6. The Pudding — `https://pudding.cool`

**Accessed:** 2026-11-16. **Category:** data journalism.

**The move.** Every story leads with a visual that *is* the argument, and a
headline written in a human voice rather than a headline voice. The page looks
like nothing else in its category because the form follows the story, not a
template.

**What to learn.** Distinctiveness comes from the artifact, not from decoration.
A page whose central object could only belong to it cannot be mistaken for a
template, however plain the surrounding typography is.

**What not to copy.** The editorial length, the scroll-driven storytelling, the
irregular layouts. Those work for a one-off story and break a page that must be
maintained.

**Ask your page:** what is the one object on this page that could only exist
here?

## 7. Craigslist — `https://www.craigslist.org`

**Accessed:** 2026-11-16. **Category:** classifieds.

**The move.** Radical subtraction. Almost no styling, dense links, no imagery, no
persuasion — and it still works, because the reader arrives with intent and the
page gets out of the way.

**What to learn.** Design is not the same as decoration, and "unstyled" is a
legitimate answer when the reader's intent is already formed. It is also the
strongest possible argument that a landing page's job is to *route intent*, not
to perform brand.

**What not to copy.** The absence of hierarchy, the dated visual language, the
assumption of intent. A cold reader needs an argument; Craigslist's reader
already has one. Confusing "no design" with "good design" is how products end up
looking abandoned.

**Ask your page:** which part of this page exists because a template had a slot
there?

## 8. Berkshire Hathaway — `https://www.berkshirehathaway.com`

**Accessed:** 2026-11-16. **Category:** the anti-landing-page.

**The move.** Plain HTML, a list of links, no imagery, no CSS. It is trusted
because the content is verifiable (letters, SEC filings, annual reports) and the
page makes no attempt to persuade. Note that the page even carries a notice about
**fraudulent claims of endorsement** — a reminder that impersonation is a live
threat for any trusted brand.

**What to learn.** Trust can come entirely from verifiability. A page that links
to primary documents is more credible than one that describes itself. And a
brand with nothing to prove can look like anything — including nothing.

**What not to copy.** The appearance. This page works because of decades of
accumulated trust and a reader who arrived to read a letter, not to evaluate a
product. For a new product, "no design" reads as "no product".

**Ask your page:** is there a primary document I could link instead of a sentence
I wrote about it?

## 9. Brilliant — `https://www.brilliant.org`

**Accessed:** 2026-11-16. **Category:** adjacent education product.

**The move.** The hero names the category; the very next block answers the
category's biggest objection — that an AI tutor just does the homework — in a
heading. It splits the audience immediately into learner and parent/teacher
paths, and stacks third-party proof (a child-safety certification, an
accreditation, a large count of five-star app reviews) before explaining the
mechanism. The mechanism is then described as pedagogy: the tutor asks guiding
questions and leads the student to the answer instead of giving it.

**What to learn.** Naming the objection in a heading is powerful when the
objection is the actual blocker — and for AI tutoring, "will this just do my
homework" is the blocker. The learner/parent split at the top is the honest
version of "three audiences": let each one choose a path instead of averaging
both into one hero.

**What not to copy.** The named AI persona (Zivon does not name its tutor), the
certification badges (they are theirs, earned, and not transferable), the review
count as a trust proxy, and the dark visual language. Also do not copy the
objection headline unless your product genuinely resolves the objection — an
objection headline over a product that does give answers is a confession.

**Ask your page:** which objection do my readers actually hold — and is it in the
headline, or buried in the FAQ?

## What none of them teach

- **Your audience.** Every page above is written for a different reader. The
  transferable part is the mechanism, never the tone.
- **Your proof.** You cannot borrow credibility. A certification, a count, or a
  logo belongs to the company that earned it.
- **Your voice.** A teardown that becomes a moodboard produces a page that looks
  like nine other pages. The point of reading them is to see the *choices*, then
  make different ones.

## Re-verification

Teardowns rot. Before citing one in a brief or a review, reload the URL and check
the five passes above. If the page has changed shape, update the entry's date and
observation in the same PR; a teardown that describes last year's page is worse
than no teardown, because it teaches a decision that no longer applies.

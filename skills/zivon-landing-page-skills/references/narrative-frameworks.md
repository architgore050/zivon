# Narrative frameworks

A landing page is a short argument with a shape. This file gives the default
shape, the classic frameworks as lenses, and the rule that keeps them from
turning into a Frankenstein page.

**The rule: one spine, several lenses.** A spine is the page's structure. A lens
sharpens one sentence inside it. Choosing two spines produces a page that reads
like two pages stapled together — the single most common failure in
framework-driven copy.

## 1. The default spine

For a cold landing page, use this. It is StoryBrand's arc `[P]` fused with a
direct-response structure, and it maps cleanly onto the argument chain in
`page-anatomy.md`.

| Beat | Job on the page | What it says | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Hook** | stop the scroll honestly | the one thing the reader wants, or the thing that is wrong | a pun, a slogan, or a question the reader does not care about |
| **2. Tension** | explain why the obvious fix does not work | "the textbook shows you a still picture of something that moves" | blaming the reader, or manufacturing a villain |
| **3. Guide** | establish that you understand and can help | empathy in one line, authority in one artifact | biography, credentials, "we are passionate about education" |
| **4. Plan** | make the next step feel small and legible | read → see it move → ask → practise → prove | a feature list disguised as steps |
| **5. Proof** | pay the claims | a real page, a real voice turn, sources, honest limits | logos of companies that never used it |
| **6. Offer** | say what the action costs and what it does not | "free, no account, one chapter" | hiding the price until the last screen |
| **7. Action** | one button, one outcome, no friction | "Open Class 10 · Light" | three buttons, a form, and a checkbox |

Length is not the measure. A beat that has nothing to say is deleted, not
padded. A strong page can compress 1–3 into a single screen and spend the rest
on 4–5.

### The heading test

Read only the section headings, top to bottom. They must form a sentence that is
the argument:

> Physics that answers back → See it move → Ask in your language → Practise
> until it is obvious → Where every claim comes from → Start with one chapter

If the headings read as a list of nouns ("Features", "Benefits", "About",
"Contact"), the spine is missing and the sections are furniture.

### The two-sentence test

If the spine works, you can summarise the page in two sentences without
referring to its layout. If you cannot, the argument is not there yet — no
amount of visual design will supply it.

## 2. The frameworks, and what each is actually for

| Framework | Original arc | Use it for | Where it lives in the spine | Danger |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **PAS** — Problem, Agitate, Solution | direct response `[P]` | a problem-aware reader who feels the pain | beats 1–3, compressed | agitation becomes fear-mongering; skip the "agitate" for a child audience |
| **AIDA** — Attention, Interest, Desire, Action | early 20th-century advertising `[P]` | checking that the page has an arc at all | the whole spine, as a diagnostic | it is a checklist, not a structure; using it as the structure produces four equal blocks |
| **StoryBrand SB7** — character, problem, guide, plan, call to action, avoid failure, reach success `[P]` | brand messaging | the guide/plan relationship; keeping the reader as the hero | beats 2–6 | the "avoid failure" beat is where children's products go wrong; use it as "what stays the same if nothing changes", not as a threat |
| **BAB** — Before, After, Bridge | direct response `[P]` | a transformation the reader can feel in one screen | beats 1 and 6 | the "after" becomes fantasy ("become a topper"); keep it small and true |
| **FAB** — Feature, Advantage, Benefit | sales training `[P]` | turning one mechanism into one reader consequence | inside a section, never as the page | three-column FAB grids are the feature dump in a costume |
| **4U** — Urgent, Unique, Useful, Ultra-specific | headline model `[P]` | pressure-testing a single headline | the hook only | "urgent" invites fake urgency; drop it for this audience |

**Attribution note:** these are practitioner models, not findings. They are
useful because they encode decades of observation about attention and sequence,
not because they have been tested against a control group. Treat them as
hypotheses with a good track record, and test your page.

## 3. Choosing the spine

| Situation | Spine | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Cold traffic, search, no prior belief | default spine, hook first | the reader needs the problem named before the product |
| Referral from a friend | proof-first variant: hook → proof → mechanism → plan → offer | trust is already lent; spend it on confirmation, not persuasion |
| Comparing tools (product-aware) | mechanism-first: hook → tension → mechanism → proof → objection → offer | differentiation is the whole job at this stage |
| Returning visitor | no spine; a resume affordance | re-selling a chosen product is friction |
| A launch or announcement | BAB with a dated proof | the news is the hook, and it must be true |

If traffic is mixed, **split the URL, not the page**. Two campaigns with two
heroes beat one page with two arguments.

## 4. Choosing one, not stacking

The Frankenpage has a signature: each section is internally well-formed and none
of them refers to the one before. You can detect it in three reads.

1. **The connector read.** Put "so" between each pair of section headings. If
   any pair cannot be joined by "so", "but", or "because", the chain is broken.
2. **The spine count.** Name the structure out loud. If you need two names
   ("it's AIDA with a StoryBrand middle"), you have two pages.
3. **The deletion read.** Delete each section. If the page still argues
   coherently, that section was a lens someone promoted to a spine.

## 5. The same promise, three spines

The promise: *a student can ask a doubt out loud, in the language they think in,
and hear an explanation anchored to the page they are reading.*

**Default spine (cold traffic):**

> **Hook:** The doubt you didn't ask.
> **Tension:** A textbook cannot answer, a class cannot pause, and a tutor costs
> more than the doubt is worth.
> **Guide:** So we made the page itself answer — out loud, in your language.
> **Plan:** Open a chapter → ask → hear it → try the check.
> **Proof:** [a real 20 s turn, transcript, the page it is anchored to]
> **Offer:** Free, no account, Class 9 and 10 live today.
> **Action:** Open a chapter.

**BAB (warm traffic, a launch post):**

> **Before:** You read the page, you nod, you close it, and the doubt is still
> there at 11 pm.
> **After:** You ask out loud, in Hinglish, and the page answers about the
> paragraph you are on.
> **Bridge:** One chapter is open now, free.

**PAS (problem-aware search traffic):**

> **Problem:** Numerical on refraction, and the diagram does not move.
> **Agitate (mildly):** Reading it again produces the same still picture.
> **Solution:** Change the angle and watch the ray bend — then ask why.

Same promise, three shapes, one honest claim. Note what did **not** change: the
specific mechanism, the language, and the absence of a threat.

## 6. The claims ledger

Every spine produces claims. Before layout, list them.

| # | Claim, as it will appear | Beat | Evidence on the page | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | "Ask out loud, in Hinglish" | 3 | the real 20 s clip + transcript | content |
| C2 | "Anchored to the page you are reading" | 3 | screenshot with the anchor chip visible | frontend |
| C3 | "Aligned to NCERT Class 10" | 5 | chapter list + source citation | content/compliance |
| C4 | "Free to start, no account" | 6 | the actual gate behaviour | product |

A claim with no evidence row is deleted or rewritten as a limitation. A claim
whose owner is "marketing" has no owner.

## 7. Anti-patterns in narrative

- **The framework stack.** AIDA hero, PAS middle, StoryBrand plan, BAB close.
- **The teaser hook.** A headline that withholds the product to create
  curiosity. The reader has 10 seconds `[E]`; curiosity is not a strategy.
- **The origin-story opener.** "We started in 2026 when…" — the reader's story
  comes first, and the maker's story belongs on `/about`.
- **The villain.** Blaming schools, teachers, or "the system" reads as an attack
  on the reader's world.
- **The false stakes.** "If you don't fix this now, your boards are gone." For a
  minor this is not persuasion; it is harm.
- **The punchline close.** Ending on a joke instead of an action.

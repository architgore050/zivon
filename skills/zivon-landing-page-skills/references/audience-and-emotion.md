# Audience and emotion

Who is actually reading the page, what they already believe, and which emotions
are honest to use. Read this before writing a single line.

Two rules frame everything below:

1. **The page speaks to one reader.** Not a demographic — a person with a
   specific belief arriving from a specific place.
2. **Emotion is honest only when it is the emotion the reader already has.**
   Manufacturing one (fear, shame, scarcity) is manipulation; naming one they
   arrived with is recognition.

## 1. Three people, one reader

| Person | Role | What they need | Where they belong |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The student** (Class 9–12) | user, and often the person who found the page | "Can this help me with the thing I am stuck on, in my language, on my phone?" | the landing page |
| **The parent** | payer, sometimes the one who forwards the link | "Is this safe, honest, and worth the money and the screen time?" | a labelled block plus a linked page — never the hero |
| **The teacher** | gatekeeper and recommender | "Does this align with the syllabus and does it teach rather than answer?" | `/for-schools`, linked from the footer |

The failure mode is a hero written for the parent ("Screen-time-safe, syllabus-
aligned learning for your child") shown to a student who wanted to understand
refraction tonight. It reads as an advertisement *about* them, and they leave.
Write for the student, and answer the parent's questions lower down, clearly
labelled.

## 2. Why they are here: five entrances

Awareness stage follows Eugene Schwartz's five levels — unaware, problem-aware,
solution-aware, product-aware, most-aware — a 1966 practitioner model that still
describes buyer readiness well `[P]`. What matters for a landing page is that
**the entrance determines the message**, and one page cannot serve all five.

| Entrance | Awareness | What they already believe | What the first screen must do | What loses them |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Search** ("refraction class 10 numericals", "NCERT class 9 motion") | problem-aware, sometimes solution-aware | "I have this exact problem, right now" | show the answer's shape and a way into it — route, do not pitch | a brand story, a signup wall, a video they must watch first |
| **A classmate's link** | solution-aware | "someone I know says this works" | confirm the friend was right, fast: the artifact, the mechanism, the proof | hype that does not match the friend's description |
| **A school or tuition WhatsApp forward** | product-aware, often read by a parent | "a teacher/parent sent this" | safety, syllabus alignment, cost, who made it | student slang, a demand to sign up before seeing anything |
| **A build-in-public post** | curious about the maker | "I want to see what this person is building" | the maker's page (`/about`, the log) — not the landing page | a landing page pretending to be a blog |
| **Returning visitor** | most-aware | "I know this; let me continue" | get out of the way: continue, resume, open the chapter | being re-sold the product they already chose |

"The first screen" below means the page that entrance actually lands on, not
necessarily `/`.

**Proposal, pending the founder's decision:** the landing page's primary reader
is entrance 2 (a classmate's link), with entrance 1 (search) routed to the
chapter pages that answer the specific query. Entrances 3, 4 and 5 get their own
surfaces (a parents/teachers page, `/about`, a resume affordance). Trying to
absorb them by adding sections is how a page becomes a brochure.

This is a positioning proposal, not a frozen decision. The frozen spec makes `/`
indexable, and the chapter pages are the SEO surface, so if brand or generic
search is expected to land on `/`, the hero and the section order change. Record
the choice in the brief's open questions before the hero is written.

Search traffic is the largest cold source and the least patient: the reader
wants the concept, not the company. Whether that reader lands on `/` or on a
chapter page, a signup wall between them and the answer converts the *wrong*
thing — it produces a bounce and a bad first impression, not a user.

## 3. Jobs to be done

Written as the student would say them, not as a marketing framework.

| Layer | Job | What the page must therefore show |
|---|---|---|
| **Functional** | "Help me understand this one concept well enough to solve the numerical." | A real lesson artifact: the diagram, the simulation, the worked step. |
| **Functional** | "Let me check whether I actually understood it." | A check with feedback, shown — not described. |
| **Functional** | "Let me revise in the language I think in." | EN / हिन्दी / Hinglish stated plainly, with a real sample. |
| **Emotional** | "Let me not feel stupid for asking." | Voice-first, no judgement, no public leaderboard, no "you're behind". |
| **Emotional** | "Let me feel I made progress today." | One small, complete thing the reader can do on the page. |
| **Social** | "Let me not be the one who asks the dumb question in class." | The unasked-doubt framing: ask privately, as many times as you need. |
| **Social** | "Let me keep up with my class." | Syllabus coverage stated honestly, including what is not ready yet. |

The social jobs are real and they are the ones most edtech exploits. Name them,
then refuse the exploitative version (see §5).

## 4. Fears and objections

Each objection is a section's reason to exist. If an objection has no section,
either the objection is not real or the page is incomplete.

| Objection, in the student's words | The doubt underneath | Where it is answered |
|---|---|---|
| "Is this CBSE? Will it help in my board exam?" | "Is this aligned or is it random YouTube?" | coverage section: NCERT chapters, class, board relevance, sources |
| "Hindi mein hai?" / "Hinglish mein samjhaoge?" | "Will I have to translate while learning?" | a real language sample in the first screen, not a badge |
| "Will it work on my phone?" | "Is this built for someone richer than me?" | performance numbers stated honestly, data-light claim, 360 px proof |
| "Is it free? What's the catch?" | "Am I about to be sold something?" | a plain cost line, including what is metered |
| "Is this just ChatGPT with a nicer skin?" | "Is there anything here I could not get free?" | the mechanism: simulations, anchored page context, review gates |
| "Will it just give me the answer?" | "Will this make me worse at exams?" | the Socratic behaviour shown, plus the teacher-facing page |
| "Is my voice recorded? Will my parents see?" | "Who is watching me?" | privacy section with specifics: what is kept, what is not, for how long |
| "Do I have to make an account?" | "How much work is trying this?" | guest-first trial, one action, no gate before value |
| "Is this for Class 9 or only 12?" | "Am I in the wrong place?" | class coverage stated before the CTA |
| "Is it any good?" | "Give me a reason to believe a stranger." | a real voice turn, real sources, an honest limitation |

**Rule:** an objection is only answered by *evidence*, never by reassurance.
"We take your privacy seriously" answers nothing. "Your audio is transcribed and
discarded; the transcript is kept only on this device unless you sign in" answers
the question.

## 5. Emotional truth: what to play to, what never to exploit

| Honest emotion | How it shows (never tells) | The exploitation to refuse |
|---|---|---|
| **Relief** — "oh, that's all it was" | one concept resolved on the page, visibly | "Struggling? You're not alone" as a hook that withholds the answer |
| **Dignity** — "I can ask this without anyone seeing" | voice-first, private, no scores shown to anyone | "Don't embarrass yourself in class" |
| **Momentum** — "I understood one thing today" | one complete, small achievement | streak pressure, "you've lost your streak" |
| **Curiosity** — "what happens if I change this?" | a simulation the reader can move | fake gamification, confetti for nothing |
| **Trust** — "someone actually made this" | sources, citations, a named maker, honest limits | borrowed authority ("IIT professors", "AI-powered") |
| **Belonging** — "this is for people like me" | language, examples, and names that are actually Indian | nationalism, "for toppers only", us-vs-them |

### The banned appeals

These are forbidden on the page, not because they are impolite but because they
are either dishonest or harmful to a child:

- **Fear of failing boards.** The fear is already there. Amplifying it to sell is
  the textbook definition of exploitation, and for a minor it is worse.
- **Shame.** No "behind your class", no "everyone else understood this", no
  ranking, no public score.
- **Parental disappointment.** Never "make your parents proud". The page is not a
  lever between a child and a parent.
- **Scarcity that is not real.** No countdowns, no "3 seats left", no
  "enrolment closes tonight" unless the system enforces it and the date is true.
- **Comparison.** No "your friends are already ahead", no leaderboard teaser.
- **Guilt.** No screen-time moralising, no "you wasted your holidays".
- **Manufactured authority.** No "IIT-designed", "official CBSE", "recommended by
  teachers" unless a named, checkable source exists.

Test: **would you be comfortable if the student's parent and teacher read this
sentence, and if the student read it five years later?** If not, it is not
honest, no matter how well it converts.

## 6. The five-second and ten-second tests

Run these before any aesthetic review. They are cheap, and they catch the
failures that no amount of polish fixes.

**Five-second test** — show the first screen for five seconds, then hide it.

1. What is this?
2. Who is it for?
3. What can you do here?
4. What does it cost?

If any answer is wrong or missing across most viewers, the hero has failed. This
is consistent with the finding that visual appeal is judged in about 50 ms while
the decision to stay is made in the first ten seconds `[E]`.

**Ten-second test** — let them read for ten seconds, then ask:

1. Say the promise back in one sentence.
2. What is the one thing that makes it different?
3. What would you have to do next?

The second question is the one that separates a landing page from a brochure.
If ten seconds of reading does not produce a differentiator, there is not one on
the page.

**Stranger read** — five people who are not building the product, three
questions each: *what is this, who is it for, what would you do next*. Record
verbatim answers. Any answer that quotes marketing language back at you means
they read the words and not the argument.

## 7. What to write down before you write the page

Fill these in, in this order, and put them at the top of the brief:

```
Reader:            <one person, one entrance>
Believes now:      <the sentence they would say on arrival>
Awareness:         <unaware | problem | solution | product | most>
Honest emotion:    <one word>
Must not exploit:  <the specific appeal we are refusing>
One action:        <the single thing the page exists to produce>
Proof available:   <what actually exists today, not what will exist>
```

If `Proof available` is empty, the page is not ready. Write the product first.

How Forces Affect Motion

What causes a change in motion — and how Newton's three laws answer every question and problem this chapter sets.

NCERT 6.1 · 6.1.1The concept of force

A force is a push or a pull. It can make a stationary object start moving, change the speed or the direction of a moving object, and even change the shape of an object — a ball starts moving when you kick it, a cricket bat changes a ball's speed and direction, a squeezed lemon changes shape.

Every force has a magnitude and a direction: change either one and the effect changes. The SI unit of force is the newton (N) — the full unit name is written lowercase ("newton"), the symbol is capitalised ("N"). A spring balance measures force; the weight of an object is the gravitational force on it. The lightest touch you can feel is only a few millinewtons.

NCERT 6.2Balanced and unbalanced forces

Most objects feel several forces at once. Two people pushing a stalled car together in the same direction add up: the net force is the sum, in the same direction. A ball floating on water has gravity pulling down and the water pushing up — equal and opposite forces that balance, so the ball's motion does not change.

When forces do not balance, the net force is the difference, pointing along the larger force. The net force is what decides what happens to the motion.

NCERT 6.3 · Activities 6.1, 6.2The force of friction

Friction is the force surfaces in contact apply on each other, opposite to the direction of motion. It is often overlooked, but it is almost always there: when you push a box, friction opposes you, and the box starts moving only when your push is larger than friction. What matters for motion is only the net force.

Friction depends on the surfaces: in the chapter's coin-and-rubber-band activity, the same twist sends the coin stack different distances on wood, laminate and marble. Hook a spring balance to a block and pull: the reading tells you the friction that surface exerts.

NCERT 6.3 Meet a Scientist · 6.4From Aristotle to the first law

For close to two thousand years it was mistakenly believed that a force is needed to keep an object moving. Galileo argued this away: if nothing opposes its motion — no friction at all — a moving object would continue to move indefinitely. Newton gave this stubbornness a name: inertia.

An object at rest remains at rest, and an object in motion continues to move with a constant velocity, unless a net force acts upon the object.

Zero net force means the object cannot start moving and cannot change its velocity — its acceleration is zero. "Constant velocity" means no change in magnitude or direction. And remember the note: even with friction zeroed, a force is needed to start motion or to stop or turn an object — but never to keep it moving.

NCERT 6.5 · Activities 6.3, 6.4The second law: F = ma

A net force produces acceleration. The chapter's cart-and-pulley activities pinned down the rules: doubling the pulling force doubled the cart's acceleration; doubling the cart's mass made the acceleration smaller. So the acceleration is in the direction of the net force, proportional to the net force, and inversely proportional to the mass:

a = F/m, that is F = ma — and 1 N is the force that gives 1 m s⁻² to a 1 kg mass (1 N = 1 kg m s⁻²).

Hold a 100 g mass in your palm: the upward force you feel is about 1 N. Earth's gravity produces g = 9.8 m s⁻² (take it as ≈10 for quick estimates), so the weight of an object is F = mg — and g does not depend on the object's mass. The chapter's "Ready to Go Beyond" adds the deeper form: momentum = mass × velocity, and it is the rate of change of momentum that a net force drives.

How the chapter's activities found F = ma
  1. Cart + pulley + hanging cup: doubling the cup's mass doubles the pull force on the cart.
  2. The cart covers the same distance from rest: s = ½aT², so a₁/a₂ = T₂²/T₁². The measured times showed the acceleration doubled when the force doubled → a ∝ F.
  3. Same force, double the cart's mass → the acceleration dropped → a falls as m grows.
  4. Combine both findings: a = F/m, i.e. F = ma, with a in the direction of the net force.

The same law explains safety tricks: a cricket player pulls her hand back while catching — a longer stopping time means a smaller deceleration and a smaller force. Airbags do the same for passengers; bubble wrap and hay protect glassware the same way. A fresh coconut cracked in one go stops in such a short time that the ground must exert a very large force — and the shell breaks.

NCERT 6.6 · Activities 6.5, 6.6, 6.7The third law: forces come in pairs

Forces arise from interactions — at least two objects. Push a ball and you feel the ball pushing you back. Two spring balances hooked together and pulled always show the same reading.

Whenever one object is exerting a force on a second object, the second object is simultaneously exerting an equal and opposite force on the first object.

The pair acts on two different objects, so it never cancels. This is how you walk: your foot pushes the ground backwards, and the ground's friction pushes you forwards — friction helps you move. Grooves in shoe soles and treads in tyres increase it; wet or icy floors remove it. A canoeist pushes water backwards with the paddle; the water pushes paddle and canoe forwards. A rocket engine expels gas downwards; the gas pushes the rocket up with a larger force than its weight, so it lifts off — and firing the engine in the direction of motion slows a rocket down, exactly how Vikram braked Chandrayaan-3 for its soft landing. The law holds for contact and non-contact forces: magnets and charged balloons obey it too.

NCERT 6.7Forces acting on a system

Two boxes of masses m₁ and m₂ tied by a string on a frictionless floor, pulled by a force F. The string's tension T is an internal force — it acts inside the system and cancels out. Treat the pair as one system, and only the external force matters:

a = F / (m₁ + m₂)

(Gravity (m₁+m₂)g still acts downwards, balanced by the normal force.) Even walking fits this idea: your arms and legs move in complex ways, but you can treat your whole body as one object.

NCERT Revise 12, 13, 14, 1Practice bench — the chapter's numericals

NCERT Revise 7, 8 · P&P 8, 9Explain these out loud

  • A sailor jumps forward off a small boat — the boat slides backwards: to launch himself forward the sailor pushes the boat backwards, and the boat's equal reaction launches him (third law).
  • A landing mat or sand bed for a high jump — it stretches the stopping time; a longer time means a smaller deceleration and a smaller force on the athlete (second law).
  • Bubble wrap or hay for glassware — the same trick: a longer collision time means a smaller force.
  • A firefighter struggling with the water pipe — the hose shoves water forwards; the water shoves the hose (and the firefighter) backwards with an equal force (third law).
Watch out — four ideas this chapter warns about
  • "A force is needed to keep an object moving." No — that is Aristotle's fallacy, and everyday friction is what fools us into believing it. With zero net force, motion continues at constant velocity.
  • "Action and reaction cancel each other." No — the pair acts on two different objects (paddle and water), so it never balances on a single object.
  • "Equal forces mean equal accelerations." No — a = F/m. The gun and the bullet feel the same 2 N but accelerate at 0.4 and 20 m s⁻².
  • "A heavier object falls with a larger acceleration." No — g = 9.8 m s⁻² does not depend on the mass of the object.

Chapter glossaryWords to keep

force
A push or a pull that can start or stop motion, change speed or direction, or change shape.
newton (N)
The SI unit of force: 1 N = 1 kg m s⁻² — the force that gives 1 m s⁻² to a 1 kg mass.
net force
The single force that stands for all the forces acting on an object together.
balanced forces
Equal and opposite forces on the same object; they do not change its motion.
force of friction
The force opposing motion between surfaces in contact, directed opposite to the motion.
inertia
An object's resistance to a change in its state of rest or motion — Newton's name for it (1687).
momentum
The product of an object's mass and its velocity.
tension
The pulling force transmitted through a string.
internal / external force
Internal forces act between parts inside a system and cancel; only external forces accelerate the system.
acceleration due to gravity (g)
9.8 m s⁻² near Earth's surface, independent of mass; weight = mg.

At a GlanceThe chapter in five lines

  • The force of friction acts on an object in the direction opposite to its direction of motion.
  • First law: an object at rest remains at rest, and an object in motion continues to move with a constant velocity, unless a net force acts upon the object.
  • Second law: a net force makes the object accelerate in the direction of the net force; the acceleration is proportional to the net force and inversely proportional to the mass. F = ma.
  • Third law: whenever one object exerts a force on a second, the second simultaneously exerts an equal and opposite force on the first.
  • For a system of connected objects, a = F/(m₁+m₂): only external forces matter.

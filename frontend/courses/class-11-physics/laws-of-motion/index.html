Laws of Motion

After this lesson you can:

  • After this lesson you can explain why Aristotle's view of motion was wrong and how Galileo's law of inertia corrected it.
  • After this lesson you can state and apply Newton's three laws of motion to simple numerical problems.
  • After this lesson you can use momentum, impulse, conservation of momentum, friction laws, and equilibrium conditions.
  • After this lesson you can analyse circular motion on a level road and on a banked road and draw free-body diagrams.

To move a football at rest, someone must kick it. To stop a rolling ball, you must apply a force against its motion. A breeze makes branches swing, and a strong wind can move heavy objects. In each case an external agency supplies a force.

The agency may touch the body, like your hand on a ball, or act from a distance. A stone released from a building accelerates downward because of the earth's gravitational pull. A bar magnet can attract an iron nail without touching it.

Aristotle held that an external force is required to keep a body in motion. That feels natural: a child must keep pulling a toy car, and the car stops when the string is released. The flaw is friction. The floor exerts friction opposite to the car's motion, and the child's pull only balances that friction.

If there were no friction, no force would be needed to keep the toy car in uniform motion. Galileo imagined this frictionless world and found the true law of motion.

A child stops pulling a toy car on a floor, and the car comes to rest. What is the correct explanation?

Why?

The car slows because friction acts opposite to its motion. If friction were absent, the car would keep moving uniformly with no net force.

Galileo studied motion on inclined planes. A body moving down an inclined plane accelerates, and a body moving up retards. Motion on a horizontal plane is the intermediate case.

Galileo concluded that an object on a frictionless horizontal plane must neither accelerate nor retard. It should move with constant velocity.

In a double inclined plane experiment, a ball released from rest on one plane rolls down and climbs the other. If the planes are smooth, the ball reaches nearly the same height, never greater. In the ideal frictionless case, the final height equals the initial height.

If the second slope is made smaller, the ball travels a longer distance to reach the same height. When the second slope is zero, the ball would travel an infinite distance and its motion would never cease. In practice friction stops it.

The state of rest and the state of uniform linear motion are equivalent: in both cases the net external force is zero. This property is called inertia, which means resistance to change.

Newton formulated Galileo's law of inertia as the first law: Every body continues to be in its state of rest or of uniform motion in a straight line unless compelled by some external force to act otherwise.

In simple terms, if the net external force on a body is zero, its acceleration is zero. Acceleration can be non-zero only if there is a net external force on the body.

There are two useful ways to apply this law. If you know the net external force is zero, you can conclude the acceleration is zero. A spaceship far in interstellar space, with its rockets off, has no net external force, so its acceleration is zero.

More often you know the object is unaccelerated, either at rest or in uniform linear motion. Then you infer that the net external force is zero. A book at rest on a table has weight W downward and normal force R upward. Because the book is observed at rest, the net force must be zero, so R equals W. It is wrong to say that W = R is the reason the book is at rest.

Inertia is felt in a bus. When the bus starts suddenly, your feet are carried forward by friction but the rest of your body tends to stay where it was, so you are thrown backward relative to the bus. When the bus stops suddenly, your feet stop but the rest of your body continues forward, so you are thrown forward.

A drop of rain falls down with a constant speed. What is the net force acting on the drop?

Why?

Constant velocity means zero acceleration. By the first law, zero acceleration means the net external force is zero.

Worked example

An astronaut accidentally gets separated out of his small spaceship accelerating in interstellar space at a constant rate of 100 m s^-2. What is the acceleration of the astronaut the instant after he is outside the spaceship? Assume that there are no nearby stars to exert gravitational force on him.

  1. Identify the forces The only possible forces on the astronaut would come from the spaceship or nearby stars. The spaceship's gravitational attraction is negligible, and there are no nearby stars.
  2. Find the net force Once the astronaut is outside the spaceship, the net external force on him is zero.
  3. Apply the first law If the net external force is zero, the acceleration is zero.

Answer The acceleration of the astronaut is zero.

Momentum of a body is the product of its mass and velocity, p = m v. Momentum is a vector. It is useful because the effect of a force depends on both mass and velocity.

A greater force is needed to push a loaded truck than a small car to the same speed in the same time. A bullet fired with high speed can pierce tissue, while the same bullet at moderate speed causes less damage. A seasoned cricketer catches a fast ball easily by drawing his hands backward, giving more time for the ball to stop.

These observations lead to Newton's second law: the rate of change of momentum of a body is directly proportional to the applied force and takes place in the direction of the force.

For a body of fixed mass, the rate of change of momentum is m a, so F = m a when the constant is chosen as 1. The SI unit of force is the newton: 1 N = 1 kg m s^-2.

The second law is a vector law and gives three component equations. It is also local: force here and now determines acceleration here and now, not from history. It applies to a particle and also to a system if F is the total external force and a is the acceleration of the system as a whole; internal forces are not included in F.

From momentum to F = m a

Start with momentum

For a body of mass m and velocity v, momentum is p = m v.

Change in momentum

If the velocity changes from v to v + Δv in time Δt, the momentum changes by Δp = m Δv because the mass is fixed.

Write the second law

The rate of change of momentum is proportional to the force: Δp/Δt is proportional to F.

Take the limit

As Δt goes to zero, Δp/Δt becomes dp/dt. With a constant k, F = k dp/dt.

Use fixed mass

For fixed mass, dp/dt = m dv/dt = m a. So F = k m a.

Choose the unit

Choose k = 1 so that F = m a. Then 1 N is the force that gives 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m s^-2.

Worked example

The motion of a particle of mass m is described by y = u t + (1/2) g t^2. Find the force acting on the particle.

  1. Differentiate position to get velocity y = u t + (1/2) g t^2, so v = dy/dt = u + g t.
  2. Differentiate velocity to get acceleration a = dv/dt = g.
  3. Apply the second law F = m a = m g.

Answer The force is F = m g, so the motion is under acceleration due to gravity.

A constant retarding force of 50 N is applied to a body of mass 20 kg moving initially with a speed of 15 m s^-1. How long does the body take to stop? Give the answer in seconds.

Why?

The retardation is a = F/m = 50/20 = 2.5 m s^-2. Using v = u + a t with final v = 0 gives 0 = 15 - 2.5 t, so t = 6 s.

Worked example

A bullet of mass 0.04 kg moving with a speed of 90 m s^-1 enters a heavy wooden block and is stopped after a distance of 60 cm. What is the average resistive force exerted by the block on the bullet?

  1. Convert distance 60 cm = 0.6 m.
  2. Find the retardation Using v^2 = u^2 + 2 a s with final v = 0: 0 = (90)^2 + 2 a (0.6). So a = -90^2/(2 × 0.6) = -6750 m s^-2.
  3. Apply the second law The magnitude of the retarding force is F = m |a| = 0.04 kg × 6750 m s^-2 = 270 N.

Answer The average resistive force is 270 N.

Sometimes a large force acts for a very short time and produces a finite change in momentum. A ball hitting a wall and bouncing back is an example. The force is large, the time is short, and the product of force and time remains measurable.

This product is called impulse: Impulse = Force × time duration = Change in momentum. A large force acting for a short time to produce a finite momentum change is called an impulsive force. Newtonian mechanics does not treat impulsive force as a separate kind of force; it is large and short-lived.

When a batsman hits a ball straight back, the ball's momentum reverses. The change in momentum gives the impulse even if the force and contact time are difficult to know separately.

Worked example

A batsman hits back a ball straight in the direction of the bowler without changing its initial speed of 12 m s^-1. If the mass of the ball is 0.15 kg, determine the impulse imparted to the ball. Assume linear motion of the ball.

  1. Choose the direction Take the initial direction from the bowler to the batsman as positive. The initial velocity is +12 m s^-1, and the final velocity is -12 m s^-1.
  2. Find the change in momentum Δp = m v_final - m v_initial = 0.15 × (-12) - 0.15 × (12) = -1.8 - 1.8 = -3.6 N s.
  3. Interpret the impulse The magnitude of the impulse is 3.6 N s. The negative sign means the impulse is in the direction from the batsman to the bowler.

Answer The impulse is 3.6 N s, in the direction from the batsman to the bowler.

Two billiard balls each of mass 0.05 kg moving in opposite directions with speed 6 m s^-1 collide and rebound with the same speed. What is the magnitude of the impulse imparted to each ball due to the other? Give the answer in N s.

Why?

For one ball, the velocity reverses from +6 m s^-1 to -6 m s^-1. Change in momentum = 0.05 × (-6) - 0.05 × (6) = -0.6 N s. The magnitude of impulse is 0.6 N s.

The second law tells how an external force changes motion, but where does the force come from? In Newtonian mechanics, the external force on a body arises due to some other body. Force is a mutual interaction between two bodies, and forces always occur in pairs.

Newton's third law states: To every action, there is always an equal and opposite reaction. More clearly, the force on body A by body B is equal and opposite to the force on body B by A.

The words action and reaction simply mean force. They do not imply that action comes before reaction; the two forces act at the same instant, and either may be called action. Action and reaction act on different bodies, so they cannot cancel each other for the motion of one body. Internal forces between parts of a system do cancel in pairs.

Which statement about action and reaction forces is correct?

Why?

Action and reaction are simultaneous mutual forces between two bodies. They are not two forces on the same body, so they do not cancel for one body's motion.

Worked example

Two identical billiard balls strike a rigid wall with the same speed but at different angles, and get reflected without any change in speed. In case (a) the ball strikes normally. In case (b) the ball strikes at 30° to the normal and reflects at 30° to the normal on the other side. What is (i) the direction of the force on the wall due to each ball? (ii) the ratio of the magnitudes of impulses imparted to the balls by the wall?

  1. Case (a): momentum change Let the initial speed be u and mass be m. Taking the normal direction as x, initial p_x = m u, final p_x = -m u, and p_y = 0 throughout. The x-component of impulse is -2 m u, and the y-component is 0.
  2. Case (b): momentum change Initial p_x = m u cos 30°, final p_x = -m u cos 30°. The y-component does not change sign. The x-component of impulse is -2 m u cos 30°, and the y-component is 0.
  3. Direction of force on the wall In both cases the impulse on the ball is along the negative x-direction, normal to the wall. By the third law, the force on the wall is normal to the wall along the positive x-direction in both cases.
  4. Ratio of impulses Ratio = (2 m u)/(2 m u cos 30°) = 1/cos 30° = 2/√3 ≈ 1.2.

Answer (i) The force on the wall is normal to the wall in both cases. (ii) The ratio of the magnitudes of impulses is 2/√3 ≈ 1.2.

The second and third laws together give an important result: the law of conservation of momentum. When a bullet is fired from a gun, the gun exerts a force on the bullet and the bullet exerts an equal and opposite force on the gun. The two forces act for the same time.

The change in momentum of the bullet is F Δt, and the change in momentum of the gun is -F Δt. Since both start from rest, the final momentum of the gun is equal and opposite to the final momentum of the bullet. The total momentum of the bullet plus gun system is conserved.

In an isolated system, mutual forces can change the momentum of individual particles, but the changes cancel in pairs. The total momentum remains unchanged.

For a collision between two bodies A and B, the final total momentum equals the initial total momentum. This is true whether the collision is elastic or inelastic. In an elastic collision there is the additional condition that total kinetic energy is also conserved.

A shell of mass 0.020 kg is fired by a gun of mass 100 kg. If the muzzle speed of the shell is 80 m s^-1, what is the recoil speed of the gun? Give the magnitude in m s^-1.

Why?

By conservation of momentum, 0.020 × 80 = 100 × v. So v = 1.6/100 = 0.016 m s^-1.

Equilibrium of a particle means the net external force on it is zero. By the first law, the particle is then at rest or in uniform motion.

If two forces act on a particle, equilibrium requires F1 = -F2, so the two forces must be equal and opposite. If three concurrent forces act, their vector sum must be zero: F1 + F2 + F3 = 0.

The resultant of any two forces must be equal and opposite to the third force. The three forces can be represented by the sides of a triangle with arrows in the same sense. For any number of forces, equilibrium means the forces can be represented by the sides of a closed polygon with arrows in the same sense.

Equation F1 + F2 + F3 = 0 also means that the x, y, and z components of the forces separately add to zero.

Worked example

A mass of 6 kg is suspended by a rope of length 2 m from the ceiling. A force of 50 N in the horizontal direction is applied at the mid-point P of the rope. What is the angle the rope makes with the vertical in equilibrium? Take g = 10 m s^-2. Neglect the mass of the rope.

  1. Find the tension supporting the weight The lower part of the rope supports the 6 kg mass. Its tension is T2 = 6 × 10 = 60 N.
  2. Resolve forces at point P At P, three forces act: T1 along the upper rope, T2 = 60 N along the lower rope, and the horizontal force 50 N. For equilibrium, horizontal and vertical components must vanish separately.
  3. Write the component equations Vertical: T1 cos θ = T2 = 60 N. Horizontal: T1 sin θ = 50 N.
  4. Solve for the angle Divide the horizontal equation by the vertical equation: tan θ = 50/60 = 5/6. So θ = tan^-1(5/6), approximately 40°.

Answer The rope makes an angle θ with the vertical where tan θ = 5/6, so θ is about 40°. The answer does not depend on the length of the rope or on the point where the horizontal force is applied.

A particle is in equilibrium under three concurrent forces F1, F2, and F3. Which condition must hold?

Why?

For equilibrium under concurrent forces, the vector sum of all forces must be zero; equivalently, each component sum is zero.

Gravity is a non-contact force that acts on every object on the earth. Most other common forces in mechanics are contact forces, because they arise when one object touches another solid or fluid.

The component of contact force normal to the surfaces is the normal reaction. The component parallel to the surfaces is friction. A solid immersed in a fluid experiences an upward buoyant force, and viscous force and air resistance are also contact forces.

A spring compressed or extended by an external force produces a restoring force F = -k x, where x is displacement and k is the force constant. The negative sign means the force is opposite to the displacement from the unstretched state. Tension is the restoring force in a string.

Static friction opposes impending relative motion and is self-adjusting up to a maximum. It satisfies f_s ≤ μ_s N, where μ_s is the coefficient of static friction. The limiting value (f_s)_max = μ_s N is independent of the area of contact.

Once relative motion starts, kinetic friction acts and satisfies f_k = μ_k N. The coefficient μ_k is less than μ_s, and kinetic friction is independent of the area of contact and nearly independent of velocity. Friction opposes relative motion, not motion itself.

On an inclined plane, a block just begins to slide at angle θ_max given by tan θ_max = μ_s. This angle is independent of the mass of the block. Rolling friction is much smaller than static or sliding friction, even by 2 or 3 orders of magnitude.

Worked example

Determine the maximum acceleration of a train in which a box lying on its floor will remain stationary, given that the coefficient of static friction between the box and the train's floor is 0.15. Take g = 10 m s^-2.

  1. Identify the force accelerating the box The box is stationary relative to the train, so it accelerates with the train. The horizontal force causing this is static friction.
  2. Write the friction limit m a = f_s ≤ μ_s N = μ_s m g.
  3. Cancel mass and solve a ≤ μ_s g. Therefore a_max = 0.15 × 10 = 1.5 m s^-2.

Answer The maximum acceleration is 1.5 m s^-2.

Worked example

A mass of 4 kg rests on a horizontal plane. The plane is gradually inclined until at an angle θ = 15° with the horizontal, the mass just begins to slide. What is the coefficient of static friction between the block and the surface?

  1. Resolve the weight Along the incline, the component of weight is mg sin θ. Perpendicular to the incline, the normal force is N = mg cos θ.
  2. Use the just-sliding condition At the angle of just sliding, static friction reaches its maximum: mg sin θ = μ_s N = μ_s mg cos θ.
  3. Solve for μ_s Cancel mg: sin θ = μ_s cos θ, so μ_s = tan θ. For θ = 15°, μ_s = tan 15° = 0.27.

Answer The coefficient of static friction is μ_s = tan 15° = 0.27. It does not depend on the mass of the block.

Worked example

A 3 kg block and a 20 kg trolley are connected by a string over a smooth pulley. The coefficient of kinetic friction between the trolley and the surface is 0.04. What is the acceleration of the system and the tension in the string? Take g = 10 m s^-2. Neglect the mass of the string.

  1. Find the friction on the trolley The normal force on the trolley is N = 20 × 10 = 200 N. Kinetic friction f_k = μ_k N = 0.04 × 200 = 8 N.
  2. Write equations for both bodies For the 3 kg block: 30 - T = 3a. For the 20 kg trolley: T - 8 = 20a. The same acceleration a applies to both because the string is inextensible and the pulley is smooth.
  3. Solve the equations Add the equations: 30 - 8 = 23a, so 22 = 23a and a = 22/23 = 0.96 m s^-2.
  4. Find the tension T = 30 - 3a = 30 - 3 × (22/23) = 27.1 N.

Answer The acceleration is 22/23 m s^-2 ≈ 0.96 m s^-2, and the tension is 27.1 N.

A box lies on the floor of a train. The train accelerates, and the box remains stationary relative to the train. Which force accelerates the box along with the train?

Why?

The box has no motion relative to the train, so static friction opposes the impending relative motion and provides the box's acceleration.

A body moving in a circle of radius R with uniform speed v has an acceleration v^2/R directed towards the centre. The force providing this acceleration is the centripetal force, f_c = m v^2 / R.

For a stone rotated by a string, the centripetal force is the tension. For a planet around the sun, it is the gravitational force. For a car on a level road, it is friction.

On a level road the vertical forces balance: N = mg. Friction provides the centripetal force, and static friction cannot exceed μ_s N. This gives the maximum speed v_max = sqrt(μ_s R g) for a car on a level road without slipping. The result is independent of the mass of the car.

Maximum speed on a level circular road

Vertical balance

There is no vertical acceleration, so N - mg = 0 and N = mg.

Friction provides centripetal force

The required centripetal force is m v^2 / R, and on a level road it is provided by static friction f_s.

Use the static friction limit

Static friction cannot exceed μ_s N = μ_s mg, so m v^2 / R ≤ μ_s mg.

Solve for maximum speed

Cancel m: v^2 / R ≤ μ_s g, so v ≤ sqrt(μ_s R g). Thus v_max = sqrt(μ_s R g).

Worked example

A cyclist speeding at 18 km/h on a level road takes a sharp circular turn of radius 3 m without reducing the speed. The coefficient of static friction between the tyres and the road is 0.1. Will the cyclist slip while taking the turn? Take g = 9.8 m s^-2.

  1. Convert the speed 18 km/h = 5 m s^-1, so v^2 = 25 m^2 s^-2.
  2. Find the friction limit For no slipping, v^2 ≤ μ_s R g. Here μ_s R g = 0.1 × 3 × 9.8 = 2.94 m^2 s^-2.
  3. Compare v^2 = 25 m^2 s^-2 is much greater than 2.94 m^2 s^-2, so the condition is not obeyed.

Answer The cyclist will slip while taking the circular turn.

On a banked road, the normal force has a horizontal component that helps provide centripetal force. The equilibrium equations are N cos θ = mg + f sin θ and N sin θ + f cos θ = m v^2 / R.

At the limiting friction, these give v_max = sqrt(R g (tan θ + μ_s)/(1 - μ_s tan θ)). This is greater than the maximum speed on a flat road.

The optimum speed is v_o = sqrt(R g tan θ). At this speed friction is not needed, so there is little wear and tear of tyres. For v < v_o, friction acts up the slope, and a car can be parked only if tan θ ≤ μ_s.

Worked example

A circular racetrack of radius 300 m is banked at an angle of 15°. If the coefficient of friction between the wheels of a race-car and the road is 0.2, what is the (a) optimum speed of the race-car to avoid wear and tear on its tyres, and (b) maximum permissible speed to avoid slipping? Take g = 9.8 m s^-2.

  1. Find the optimum speed v_o = sqrt(R g tan θ) = sqrt(300 × 9.8 × tan 15°). Using tan 15° = 0.2679 gives v_o = 28.1 m s^-1.
  2. Write the maximum speed formula v_max = sqrt(R g (tan θ + μ_s)/(1 - μ_s tan θ)).
  3. Substitute the values R g = 300 × 9.8 = 2940 m^2 s^-2. tan θ + μ_s = 0.2679 + 0.2 = 0.4679. 1 - μ_s tan θ = 1 - 0.2 × 0.2679 = 0.9464.
  4. Compute the maximum speed v_max = sqrt(2940 × 0.4679 / 0.9464) = sqrt(1453.5) ≈ 38.1 m s^-1.

Answer (a) The optimum speed is about 28.1 m s^-1. (b) The maximum permissible speed is about 38.1 m s^-1.

A stone tied to a string is whirled in a circle. The string breaks suddenly. Which trajectory does the stone follow immediately after the string breaks?

Why?

When the string breaks, the inward force disappears. By inertia, the stone continues with the velocity it had at that instant, which is along the tangent to the circle.

A typical mechanics problem involves several bodies exerting forces on one another. You may choose any part of the assembly as your system, provided you include all forces on that part due to the rest of the assembly. The remaining part is the environment.

Draw a diagram showing the parts, links, and supports. Choose a convenient system. Then draw a free-body diagram: show the system and all forces on it by the environment and other agencies. Do not show forces exerted by the system on the environment.

Include forces whose magnitudes and directions are known or sure, such as the direction of tension along a string. Treat the rest as unknowns to be found using the laws of motion. If you then choose another system, use Newton's third law: if the force on A by B is F, the force on B by A is -F.

An action-reaction pair consists of mutual forces between two bodies. Two equal and opposite forces on the same body are not an action-reaction pair. This rule helps you avoid mistakes when you identify forces.

Worked example

A wooden block of mass 2 kg rests on a soft horizontal floor. When an iron cylinder of mass 25 kg is placed on top of the block, the floor yields steadily and the block and the cylinder together go down with an acceleration of 0.1 m s^-2. What is the action of the block on the floor (a) before and (b) after the floor yields? Take g = 10 m s^-2. Identify the action-reaction pairs in the problem.

  1. Before the floor yields The block is at rest. Its free-body diagram has weight 2 × 10 = 20 N downward and normal force R upward. By the first law, R = 20 N. By the third law, the block pushes the floor downward with 20 N.
  2. After the floor yields: choose the system Choose the block plus cylinder as the system. Its total mass is 27 kg, so its weight is 270 N. The floor exerts normal force R' upward. Internal forces between block and cylinder are not shown.
  3. Apply the second law The system accelerates downward at 0.1 m s^-2. Taking downward as positive: 270 - R' = 27 × 0.1 = 2.7 N. So R' = 267.3 N.
  4. Use the third law The system pushes the floor downward with 267.3 N. Action-reaction pairs include the earth-system gravitational pair, the floor-system normal pair, and the block-cylinder mutual forces.

Answer (a) The block pushes the floor down with 20 N. (b) The block plus cylinder pushes the floor down with 267.3 N.

A man of mass 70 kg stands on a weighing scale in a lift moving downwards with a uniform acceleration of 5 m s^-2. Take g = 10 m s^-2. What is the reading of the scale in newtons?

Why?

Taking downward as positive: mg - R = m a, so R = m(g - a) = 70 × (10 - 5) = 350 N.

A body of mass 5 kg is acted upon by two perpendicular forces 8 N and 6 N. What is the magnitude of its acceleration in m s^-2?

Why?

The resultant force is sqrt(8^2 + 6^2) = 10 N. Then a = F/m = 10/5 = 2 m s^-2.

A rocket with a lift-off mass of 20,000 kg is blasted upwards with an initial acceleration of 5.0 m s^-2. Calculate the initial thrust of the blast. Take g = 10 m s^-2. Give the answer in newtons.

Why?

Taking upward as positive: thrust - mg = ma, so thrust = m(g + a) = 20,000 × (10 + 5) = 300,000 N.

Two masses 8 kg and 12 kg are connected at the two ends of a light inextensible string that goes over a frictionless pulley. Find the acceleration of the masses when they are released. Take g = 10 m s^-2. Give the answer in m s^-2.

Why?

For an Atwood machine, a = (m2 - m1)g/(m1 + m2) = (12 - 8) × 10/(8 + 12) = 40/20 = 2 m s^-2.

Passengers are thrown forward from their seats when a speeding bus stops suddenly. Why?

Why?

When the bus stops, friction stops the feet, but the rest of the body tends to keep moving forward because of inertia.

A pebble is thrown vertically upwards. Ignore air resistance. At the highest point, its velocity is momentarily zero. What is true about the force and acceleration at that instant?

Why?

A momentary velocity of zero does not mean force or acceleration is zero. At the highest point the only force is weight mg, so acceleration is g.

Some mechanics problems give you a graph of position against time instead of a list of forces. Consider such a problem for a particle of mass 4 kg in one-dimensional motion: it asks for the force on the particle for t < 0, for 0 < t < 4 s and for t > 4 s, and for the impulse at t = 0 and at t = 4 s.

To handle this, recall how position, velocity and acceleration are related (as in Example 4.3). Velocity is the rate of change of the position coordinate, and acceleration is the rate of change of velocity. In the graph, a stretch where the position does not change at all means the particle is at rest there, and a stretch where the position changes at a steady rate means the particle is in uniform motion there.

In both kinds of stretch the acceleration is zero. By the first law, zero acceleration means the net external force on the particle is zero. So the force asked for during those intervals is zero, whatever the mass of the particle is.

A net force is needed only where the velocity changes, that is, at the instants where the steady rate of change of position alters. At such an instant the second law gives F = m a, with m = 4 kg taken from the problem.

The force at such an instant acts for a very short time, so the measurable quantity is the impulse. Impulse = Force × time duration = change in momentum, and the change in momentum is found from the velocity just before and the velocity just after that instant.

The position-time graph of a particle of mass 4 kg is flat over an interval — the position does not change at all during that interval. What is the net external force on the particle during that interval?

Why?

If the position does not change, the particle is at rest. Its velocity is zero and stays zero, so its acceleration is zero. The first law then says the net external force must be zero.

In a position-time graph problem, the steady rate of change of position alters suddenly at t = 4 s. Which statement about that instant is correct?

Why?

Impulse = Force × time duration = change in momentum. A large force acting for a very short time changes the momentum, and the change is computed from the velocities just before and just after the instant.

Glossary

force
A push or pull exerted on a body by some external agency, which may touch the body or act from a distance.
inertia
A body's resistance to any change in its state of rest or uniform motion.
momentum
The product of mass and velocity of a body; a vector quantity.
impulse
Force multiplied by the time for which it acts; equal to the change in momentum.
equilibrium
The state in which the net external force on a particle is zero.
friction
The part of the contact force parallel to two surfaces that opposes impending or actual relative motion.
normal reaction
The part of the contact force perpendicular to the surfaces in contact.
centripetal force
The inward force that provides the acceleration for circular motion; it is a name for a material force such as tension, gravity, or friction.

Laws of Motion

After this lesson you can:

  • After this lesson you can explain why Aristotle's idea that a force is needed to keep a body moving is wrong, and state Galileo's law of inertia.
  • After this lesson you can state and apply Newton's three laws of motion, including momentum, impulse and the conservation of momentum.
  • After this lesson you can analyse equilibrium, friction on level and inclined surfaces, and the circular motion of a car on level and banked roads.
  • After this lesson you can draw a free-body diagram and solve multi-body problems with the laws of motion.

Imagine a football lying still on the ground. To make it move, someone has to kick it. To send a stone upward, someone has to push it up. A breeze makes the branches of a tree swing, and a strong wind can even move heavy objects. In every one of these cases some outside agency supplies a force.

Sometimes the agency touches the body, as your hand touches the ball or a flowing stream pushes a boat. This is not always necessary. A stone released from the top of a building speeds up downward because of the gravitational pull of the earth, and a bar magnet can attract an iron nail from a distance. So an external agency may act in contact or from a distance.

Force is needed to stop motion too: you can stop a ball rolling down an inclined plane by pushing it against the direction of its motion.

Now a sharper question. Suppose a body is already moving uniformly - a skater moving straight with constant speed on a horizontal ice slab. Is an external force required to keep it in uniform motion? This question looks simple, but it took ages to answer.

Aristotle (384 B.C. - 322 B.C.) held that if a body is moving, something external is required to keep it moving. On this view an arrow shot from a bow keeps flying because the air behind it keeps pushing it. Put as a law: an external force is required to keep a body in motion.

This feels natural from daily experience. A small child dragging a toy-car knows that the string must be pulled all the time; release it and the car comes to rest. Left to themselves, all bodies on the earth seem to stop.

The flaw is friction. The moving toy-car stops because the floor exerts a force of friction on it, opposite to its motion. To keep the car going, the child must pull forward with an equal force, so that the net external force on the car is zero. If there were no friction, no force at all would be needed to keep the car moving uniformly.

Opposing forces such as friction between solids, and viscous forces in fluids, are always present in the natural world. That is why we normally have to push things to keep them moving. To find the true law of nature, Galileo imagined a world with no friction at all.

Galileo studied the motion of objects on inclined planes. An object moving down an inclined plane accelerates; an object moving up it retards. Motion on a horizontal plane is the in-between case. Galileo concluded that an object moving on a frictionless horizontal plane must neither accelerate nor retard: it moves with constant velocity.

His second experiment used a double inclined plane. A ball released from rest on one plane rolls down and climbs the other. If the planes are smooth, the ball reaches nearly the same height as it started from - a little less, never more. With friction absent, the final height would equal the starting height.

Make the second plane flatter and repeat: the ball still climbs to the same height, so it must travel farther. In the limiting case the second plane is horizontal and the ball travels an infinite distance - its motion never ceases.

In practice the ball does stop after a finite distance, because friction can never be totally removed. Galileo's insight was that the state of rest and the state of uniform linear motion are equivalent: in both cases there is no net force on the body. The property a body has of resisting a change in its state of rest or uniform motion is called inertia. Inertia means 'resistance to change'.

A child drags a toy-car by a string along a floor. The car moves in a straight line with constant speed. Which statement is correct?

Why?

Constant velocity means zero acceleration, so by the first law the net external force must be zero. The child's pull and friction are equal and opposite. With no friction, no pull would be needed at all - which is exactly where Aristotle went wrong.

In Galileo's double inclined plane experiment the planes are smooth. What happens as the slope of the second plane is made flatter and flatter?

Why?

The ball always climbs back to nearly its starting height. A flatter plane means it needs more distance to reach that height, and on a horizontal plane the required distance is infinite - the idealised motion never ceases.

Newton built on Galileo's ideas and laid the foundation of mechanics in three laws of motion. Galileo's law of inertia became the first law: every body continues to be in its state of rest or of uniform motion in a straight line unless compelled by some external force to act otherwise.

Rest and uniform motion in a straight line both mean zero acceleration. So the first law can be said in one line: if the net external force on a body is zero, its acceleration is zero. Acceleration can be non-zero only if there is a net external force on the body.

Two kinds of situation arise. Sometimes we know the net force is zero - for example a spaceship far from all other objects with all its rockets turned off; then its acceleration must be zero, and if it is moving it keeps a uniform velocity. More often we do not know all the forces, but we can see that the object is at rest or in uniform motion; the first law then tells us that the net external force on it must be zero.

Take a book at rest on a table. Two forces act on it: its weight W downward and the normal force R of the table upward. The correct reasoning is not 'since W = R the forces cancel and the book stays at rest'. The correct reasoning is: the book is observed to be at rest, so by the first law the net external force on it must be zero, and therefore R must be equal and opposite to W. Gravity is everywhere and friction is common, so on the earth an object at rest or in uniform motion has forces on it that add up to zero - not no forces at all.

You feel the first law in a bus. When the bus starts suddenly you are thrown backward: your feet move with the floor because of friction, while the rest of your body tends to stay where it was, because of inertia. When the bus stops suddenly your feet stop and the rest of your body keeps moving forward, so you are thrown forward.

A drop of rain is falling vertically downward with a constant speed. What is the net external force on the drop?

Why?

Constant speed in a straight line means zero acceleration, so by the first law the net external force must be zero. The weight is balanced by the upward drag of the air. The forces are not individually zero - they cancel.

The second law relates the net external force on a body to its acceleration, and it is stated in terms of momentum. The momentum of a body is defined as the product of its mass m and its velocity v, written p = m v. Momentum is a vector quantity; it points along the velocity.

Why this product? A loaded truck needs a much greater force than a small car to reach the same speed in the same time, and a greater opposing force to stop in the same time at the same speed. A heavy stone is harder to catch than a light one dropped from the same height. A bullet fired at high speed can pierce tissue, while the same bullet at moderate speed does little damage. Mass alone, or speed alone, is not enough - the product m v is the quantity that matters.

If a fixed force acts for a certain interval of time on two bodies of different masses, initially at rest, the lighter body picks up the greater speed; but at the end of that time each body has acquired the same momentum. The same force for the same time causes the same change in momentum for different bodies.

Direction matters too. A stone whirled in a horizontal circle at constant speed has a momentum of fixed magnitude whose direction keeps changing, and a force is needed to produce that change. Your hand must pull harder if you whirl the stone faster or in a smaller circle. So the greater the rate of change of the momentum vector, the greater the force.

From momentum to F = m a

Step 1: momentum

For a body of mass m moving with velocity v, the momentum is p = m v.

Step 2: the law in words

The rate of change of momentum of a body is directly proportional to the applied force and takes place in the direction in which the force acts.

Step 3: write it in symbols

If a force F acts for a time interval Δt and the velocity changes from v to v + Δv, the momentum changes by Δp = m Δv. The law gives F ∝ Δp/Δt, that is F = k Δp/Δt with k a constant of proportionality.

Step 4: take the limit

Let Δt become very small. Then Δp/Δt becomes the derivative dp/dt, so F = k dp/dt.

Step 5: fixed mass

For a body of fixed mass, dp/dt = d(m v)/dt = m dv/dt = m a. So F = k m a.

Step 6: choose k = 1

The unit of force has not been defined yet, so we are free to choose k = 1. Then F = dp/dt = m a. In SI units the force that gives a mass of 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m s-2 is one newton, so 1 N = 1 kg m s-2.

Four points about the second law. First, putting F = 0 gives a = 0, so the second law agrees with the first. Second, it is a vector law: it is three equations, one for each component (F_x = dp_x/dt = m a_x, and similarly for y and z). A force not parallel to the velocity changes only the component of velocity along the force; the component perpendicular to the force stays unchanged - which is why a projectile under vertical gravity keeps its horizontal velocity component.

Third, the law applies to a single point particle, and in the same form to a rigid body or a system of particles; then F is the total external force on the system and a is the acceleration of the system as a whole, with internal forces left out. Fourth, the law is a local relation: the force at a point at an instant determines the acceleration at that point at that instant. A stone dropped out of an accelerating train has, the moment after release, no horizontal force and no horizontal acceleration - it carries no memory of the train's acceleration.

Remember also what F means. In F = m a, F is the net force due to all material agencies external to the body, and a is the effect of that force. The product m a is not one more force to be added to the list.

Worked example

An astronaut accidentally gets separated out of his small spaceship accelerating in interstellar space at a constant rate of 100 m s-2. What is the acceleration of the astronaut the instant after he is outside the spaceship? (Assume that there are no nearby stars to exert gravitational force on him.)

  1. List the forces There are no nearby stars to pull him, and the small spaceship exerts negligible gravitational attraction on him.
  2. Net force So the net external force on the astronaut, once he is out of the spaceship, is zero.
  3. Apply the first law If the net external force on a body is zero, its acceleration is zero.

Answer The acceleration of the astronaut is zero. He does not keep the spaceship's 100 m s-2 acceleration, because force is not 'carried' by a body from its earlier motion.

Worked example

A bullet of mass 0.04 kg moving with a speed of 90 m s-1 enters a heavy wooden block and is stopped after a distance of 60 cm. What is the average resistive force exerted by the block on the bullet?

  1. Find the retardation Assume the retardation a is constant. From the speed u = 90 m s-1 and the stopping distance s = 60 cm = 0.6 m, a = u²/(2s) = (90 × 90)/(2 × 0.6) m s-2 = 6750 m s-2.
  2. Apply the second law The retarding force is F = m a = 0.04 kg × 6750 m s-2.

Answer F = 270 N. The actual resistive force, and therefore the retardation, may not be uniform, so this answer only indicates the average resistive force.

Worked example

The motion of a particle of mass m is described by y = u t + ½ g t². Find the force acting on the particle.

  1. Velocity Differentiate the position with respect to time: v = dy/dt = u + g t.
  2. Acceleration Differentiate again: a = dv/dt = g.
  3. Force Apply the second law: F = m a = m g.

Answer F = m g. The equation describes a particle moving under the acceleration due to gravity, with y the position coordinate measured in the direction of g.

Some forces act for a very short time but are large enough to change momentum a lot - a ball hitting a wall and bouncing back, for example. The force on the ball acts only while ball and wall are in contact, and the force and the contact time are hard to measure separately. Their product is not hard: it is the change in momentum.

That product is called impulse: impulse = force × time duration = change in momentum. A large force acting for a short time to produce a finite change in momentum is called an impulsive force. In Newtonian mechanics an impulsive force is not a separate kind of force - it is simply large and short-lived.

This explains a familiar skill. A seasoned cricketer draws his hands backward while catching, so the ball takes a longer time to stop. The same change of momentum spread over a longer time needs a smaller force, so his hands are not hurt. A novice who holds his hands fixed must stop the ball almost instantly, needs a much larger force, and gets hurt.

Worked example

A batsman hits back a ball straight in the direction of the bowler without changing its initial speed of 12 m s-1. If the mass of the ball is 0.15 kg, determine the impulse imparted to the ball. (Assume linear motion of the ball.)

  1. Choose a positive direction Take the direction from the bowler to the batsman as positive. Initial momentum = 0.15 × 12 = 1.8 kg m s-1.
  2. Final momentum After the hit the ball moves back with the same speed, so its final momentum is −1.8 kg m s-1.
  3. Change in momentum Change in momentum = 0.15 × 12 − (−0.15 × 12) = 3.6 N s, and impulse equals this change.

Answer Impulse = 3.6 N s, directed from the batsman to the bowler. The force on the ball and the time of contact are hard to know here, but the impulse is easy to calculate.

A constant retarding force of 50 N is applied to a body of mass 20 kg moving initially with a speed of 15 m s-1. How long does the body take to stop? Give the answer in seconds.

Why?

The retardation is a = F/m = 50/20 = 2.5 m s-2. That means the body loses 2.5 m s-1 of speed every second, so its 15 m s-1 is used up in 15/2.5 = 6 s.

The second law tells you what an external force does; the third law tells you where such a force comes from. In Newtonian mechanics the external force on a body always arises from some other body. Press a coiled spring and the spring pushes back on your hand. The earth pulls a stone down, and the stone pulls the earth up with an equal and opposite force, which we do not notice because the earth is so massive.

So force never occurs singly: force is a mutual interaction between two bodies, and forces always occur in pairs. Newton stated it as: to every action there is always an equal and opposite reaction. In symbols, the force on A by B equals minus the force on B by A: F_AB = −F_BA.

Three warnings. The words action and reaction mean nothing more than 'force'. There is no cause-and-effect order in them - the two forces act at the same instant, and either may be called action. And the two forces act on different bodies, so if you are studying the motion of one body, only one of the pair is relevant; adding them and claiming the net force is zero is an error. Only when both bodies are treated as one system do the pair become internal forces and cancel.

Worked example

Two identical billiard balls strike a rigid wall with the same speed but at different angles, and get reflected without any change in speed. In case (a) a ball strikes the wall normally and bounces straight back. In case (b) a ball strikes the wall with its velocity at 30° to the normal and is reflected at 30° to the normal on the other side. What is (i) the direction of the force on the wall due to each ball, and (ii) the ratio of the magnitudes of the impulses imparted to the balls by the wall?

  1. Set up axes Let u be the speed of each ball before and after the collision and m its mass. Take x along the normal to the wall, away from the wall as negative, and y along the wall.
  2. Case (a): normal hit Initial p_x = m u, p_y = 0; final p_x = −m u, p_y = 0. So the x-component of impulse is −2 m u and the y-component is 0.
  3. Case (b): hit at 30° to the normal Initial p_x = m u cos 30°, p_y = −m u sin 30°; final p_x = −m u cos 30°, p_y = −m u sin 30°. Only p_x changes sign, so the x-component of impulse is −2 m u cos 30° and the y-component is 0.
  4. Use the third law Impulse and force are in the same direction. In both cases the impulse on the ball is normal to the wall; by the third law the force on the wall due to the ball is normal to the wall, in both cases.

Answer The force on the wall is normal to the wall in both cases. The ratio of the impulse magnitudes is 2 m u /(2 m u cos 30°) = 2/√3, about 1.2. The magnitude of the force cannot be found, because the small contact time is not given.

A book rests on a table. Which of the following is an action-reaction pair in the sense of Newton's third law?

Why?

An action-reaction pair is the two mutual forces between the same two bodies - here the book and the earth. The weight of the book and the normal force on it are two forces on the same body; they happen to be equal and opposite because the book is at rest, but they are not a third-law pair.

The second and third laws together give the law of conservation of momentum. Fire a bullet from a gun. The force on the bullet by the gun is F; by the third law the force on the gun by the bullet is −F; both act for the same time interval. So the change in momentum of the bullet is F times that interval, and that of the gun is the opposite. Both start from rest, so their final momenta are equal and opposite, and the total momentum of the (bullet + gun) system stays zero.

In an isolated system - one with no external force - the mutual forces between pairs of particles change the momentum of individual particles, but for each pair the changes are equal and opposite and cancel. So the total momentum remains unchanged. The law of conservation of momentum states: the total momentum of an isolated system of interacting particles is conserved.

Collisions are the standard example. Two bodies A and B with initial momenta p_A and p_B collide and separate with momenta p′_A and p′_B. By the second law, using a common time of contact, F_AB times that time = p′_A − p_A and F_BA times that time = p′_B − p_B. Since F_AB = −F_BA, we get p′_A + p′_B = p_A + p_B: the final total momentum equals the initial total momentum. This is true whether the collision is elastic or inelastic.

Worked example

A shell of mass 0.020 kg is fired by a gun of mass 100 kg. If the muzzle speed of the shell is 80 m s-1, what is the recoil speed of the gun?

  1. Before firing Shell and gun are both at rest, so the total momentum of the system is zero.
  2. Conservation of momentum There is no external force along the barrel, so the total momentum stays zero: mass of shell × speed of shell + mass of gun × speed of gun = 0.
  3. Solve 0.020 × 80 = 100 × v_gun, so v_gun = 1.6/100.

Answer The gun recoils with a speed of 0.016 m s-1, in the direction opposite to the shell.

Two billiard balls, each of mass 0.05 kg, move in opposite directions with speed 6 m s-1, collide and rebound with the same speed. What is the magnitude of the impulse imparted to each ball due to the other? Give the answer in N s.

Why?

Take one ball's initial direction as positive. Its momentum changes from 0.05 × 6 = 0.3 kg m s-1 to −0.3 kg m s-1. The change is 0.6 kg m s-1, so the impulse is 0.6 N s, directed opposite to that ball's original motion.

A particle is in equilibrium in mechanics when the net external force on it is zero. By the first law, that means the particle is either at rest or in uniform motion.

Under two forces F1 and F2, equilibrium requires F1 = −F2: the two forces must be equal and opposite. Under three concurrent forces F1, F2 and F3, equilibrium requires F1 + F2 + F3 = 0. The resultant of any two of them, found by the parallelogram law, must be equal and opposite to the third; the three forces can be drawn as the sides of a triangle with the arrows taken in the same sense.

The result generalises to any number of forces: they keep a particle in equilibrium if they can be represented by the sides of a closed n-sided polygon with arrows in the same sense. In components, each of the x, y and z sums must vanish separately.

Worked example

A mass of 6 kg is suspended by a rope of length 2 m from the ceiling. A force of 50 N in the horizontal direction is applied at the mid-point P of the rope. What is the angle the rope makes with the vertical in equilibrium? (Take g = 10 m s-2.) Neglect the mass of the rope.

  1. Look at the suspended weight The weight is in equilibrium under its own weight and the tension T2 in the lower part of the rope, so T2 = 6 × 10 = 60 N.
  2. Look at point P Three forces act on P: the tension T1 along the upper part of the rope, the tension T2 = 60 N along the lower part (vertically down), and the horizontal force of 50 N.
  3. Resolve at P The vertical components must cancel: T1 cos θ = T2 = 60 N. The horizontal components must cancel: T1 sin θ = 50 N.
  4. Solve Divide the two equations: tan θ = 50/60 = 0.83, so θ is about 40°. Then T1 = √(60² + 50²) ≈ 78 N.

Answer The rope makes an angle of about 40° with the vertical. The answer does not depend on the length of the rope (assumed massless) or on the point at which the horizontal force is applied.

Three concurrent forces act on a particle that is in equilibrium. Which statement must be true?

Why?

Equilibrium of a particle means zero net external force, so F1 + F2 + F3 = 0, and each component sum vanishes separately. The particle may also be in uniform motion, not necessarily at rest.

Several kinds of force appear again and again in mechanics. The gravitational force is pervasive: every object on the earth feels the earth's pull, and gravity also governs the motion of celestial bodies. It acts at a distance, without any medium in between.

All the other common forces are contact forces, arising when an object touches another object, solid or fluid. When two bodies are in contact there are mutual contact forces satisfying the third law. The component of the contact force normal to the surfaces in contact is called the normal reaction; the component parallel to the surfaces is called friction.

Contact forces also arise between a solid and a fluid: a solid immersed in a fluid feels an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of the fluid displaced, and viscous force and air resistance are contact forces too. Tension in a string and the force of a spring are further examples. A spring compressed or stretched by an amount x exerts a restoring force F = −k x, where k is the force constant; the minus sign says the force is opposite to the displacement from the unstretched state.

For an inextensible string the force constant is very high and the restoring force is called tension. For a string of negligible mass the tension T may be taken as the same throughout the string. At the microscopic level all these contact forces come from electrical forces between the charged constituents of the bodies, but their detailed origin is complex, so in mechanics they are treated as separate kinds of force whose properties are found by experiment.

Put a block on a horizontal table. Gravity mg is cancelled by the normal reaction N. Now apply a small horizontal force F. The block may not move at all - yet if F were the only horizontal force it would have to accelerate. So another horizontal force must oppose F, keeping the net force zero. That force, parallel to the surface, is friction.

While the block stays at rest, the friction is static friction f_s. It does not exist by itself: it appears the moment an applied force appears, and it grows to stay equal and opposite to the applied force. Static friction opposes impending motion - motion that would take place if friction were absent.

Static friction has a limit. Experiment shows that the limiting value (f_s)max is independent of the area of contact and varies with the normal force as (f_s)max = µ_s N, where µ_s is the coefficient of static friction, a constant depending only on the nature of the two surfaces. So f_s ≤ µ_s N. When the applied force exceeds (f_s)max the block begins to slide, and the friction drops to a smaller value: kinetic (sliding) friction f_k = µ_k N, again independent of the area of contact and nearly independent of velocity, with µ_k less than µ_s.

While sliding, the acceleration of the block is (F − f_k)/m; at constant velocity F = f_k exactly; remove the applied force and the acceleration is − f_k/m until the block stops. Notice that friction opposes relative motion, not motion: a box lying in the floor of an accelerating train is accelerated forward by static friction, and stays at rest relative to the train.

A ring or sphere rolling without slipping would in principle feel no friction at all, since its single point of contact has no relative motion with the plane. In practice some rolling friction occurs, and for the same weight it is much smaller than static or sliding friction, even by 2 or 3 orders of magnitude - which is why the wheel has been such a milestone. Lubricants, ball bearings and a thin cushion of air reduce friction, but friction is also essential: brakes use kinetic friction to stop machines and vehicles, and we can walk only because of friction.

Worked example

A mass of 4 kg rests on a horizontal plane. The plane is gradually inclined until at an angle θ = 15° with the horizontal the mass just begins to slide. What is the coefficient of static friction between the block and the surface?

  1. Forces on the block Its weight mg acting vertically downward, the normal force N of the plane, and the static frictional force f_s opposing the impending motion.
  2. Resolve the weight Along the slope the weight component is mg sin θ; perpendicular to the slope it is mg cos θ. In equilibrium mg sin θ = f_s and mg cos θ = N.
  3. At the point of sliding As θ increases, the self-adjusting f_s increases until at θ_max it reaches its maximum value µ_s N. Then mg sin θ_max = µ_s mg cos θ_max.
  4. The friction law for an incline Dividing gives tan θ_max = µ_s, and θ_max depends only on µ_s, not on the mass of the block.
  5. Insert the numbers For θ_max = 15°, µ_s = tan 15° = 0.27.

Answer µ_s = 0.27. Slightly beyond 15°, the small net force down the slope makes the block begin to slide.

Worked example

A box lies on the floor of a train. The coefficient of static friction between the box and the floor is 0.15. Determine the maximum acceleration of the train for which the box will remain stationary. (Take g = 10 m s-2.)

  1. Which force accelerates the box? If the box is stationary relative to the train, it accelerates along with the train, and the only horizontal force available to do this is static friction.
  2. Second law for the box m a = f_s, and static friction cannot exceed µ_s N = µ_s m g.
  3. Inequality So m a ≤ µ_s m g, that is a ≤ µ_s g.

Answer a_max = µ_s g = 0.15 × 10 m s-2 = 1.5 m s-2.

Worked example

A 3 kg block hangs from a string that passes over a smooth pulley and is tied to a 20 kg trolley standing on a horizontal surface. The coefficient of kinetic friction between the trolley and the surface is 0.04. Find the acceleration of the system and the tension in the string. (Take g = 10 m s-2. Neglect the mass of the string.)

  1. One acceleration The string is inextensible and the pulley smooth, so the 3 kg block and the 20 kg trolley have the same magnitude of acceleration a.
  2. Equation for the block The block weighs 30 N and moves down: 30 − T = 3a.
  3. Equation for the trolley T − f_k = 20a, with f_k = µ_k N = 0.04 × (20 × 10) = 8 N. So T − 8 = 20a.
  4. Add and solve Adding the two equations: 30 − 8 = 23a, so a = 22/23 m s-2.
  5. Tension T = 8 + 20a = 27.1 N.

Answer a = 22/23 m s-2 ≈ 0.96 m s-2, and T ≈ 27.1 N.

You push a block lying on a rough horizontal table with a small horizontal force. The block does not move. What is the friction force on the block at that instant?

Why?

Static friction is self-adjusting: it grows to match the applied force, up to the limit µ_s N. Only when the block is just about to slide does f_s equal µ_s N. Kinetic friction acts only after sliding has begun.

A body moving in a circle of radius R with uniform speed v has an acceleration v²/R directed towards the centre. By the second law, the force providing this acceleration is f_c = m v²/R, also directed towards the centre. This force is called the centripetal force.

Centripetal force is not a new kind of force. It is only a name for whichever real force happens to point towards the centre: for a stone whirled on a string it is the tension in the string, for a planet going round the sun it is the gravitational pull of the sun, and for a car turning on a horizontal road it is the friction between the tyres and the road.

Maximum speed of a car on a level road

Step 1: the three forces

Three forces act on the car: its weight mg, the normal reaction N, and friction f.

Step 2: no vertical acceleration

N − mg = 0, so N = mg.

Step 3: who supplies the centripetal force?

The centripetal force must act along the road, towards the centre of the turn. The only horizontal force available is friction, so f = m v²/R.

Step 4: friction has a limit

Static friction satisfies f ≤ µ_s N = µ_s m g.

Step 5: combine

m v²/R ≤ µ_s m g gives v² ≤ µ_s R g. The mass cancels, so the answer is independent of the mass of the car.

Maximum safe speed on a level circular turn

On a level (unbanked) road friction alone supplies the centripetal force, so the maximum safe speed is v_max = √(µ_s R g). The plot draws this curve of v_max against the turn radius R for the coefficient of static friction you choose, and prints the value at your chosen radius. Take g = 10 m s-2.

On a banked road the outer edge of the turn is raised, so the road surface makes an angle θ with the horizontal. The normal reaction then has a horizontal component pointing towards the centre, and friction has less work to do.

There is still no acceleration in the vertical direction, so the vertical forces must cancel: N cos θ = mg + f sin θ. The centripetal force is supplied by the horizontal components of N and f together: N sin θ + f cos θ = m v²/R.

To find the maximum permissible speed, put friction at its limit, f = µ_s N, and solve these two equations together.

Maximum and optimum speed on a banked road

Step 1: put f at its limit

Set f = µ_s N in the two banked-road equations: N cos θ = mg + µ_s N sin θ, and N sin θ + µ_s N cos θ = m v²/R.

Step 2: find N

From the first equation, N = mg/(cos θ − µ_s sin θ).

Step 3: substitute

Putting this N into the second equation and simplifying gives v²_max = R g (tan θ + µ_s)/(1 − µ_s tan θ).

Step 4: compare with the level road

The maximum possible speed of a car on a banked road is greater than that on a flat road.

Step 5: switch friction off

With µ_s = 0 the result becomes v_o = √(R g tan θ). At this optimum speed friction is not needed at all, so driving at it causes little wear and tear of the tyres. For v < v_o friction acts up the slope, and a car can be parked only if tan θ ≤ µ_s.

Worked example

A cyclist speeding at 18 km/h on a level road takes a sharp circular turn of radius 3 m without reducing the speed. The coefficient of static friction between the tyres and the road is 0.1. Will the cyclist slip while taking the turn? (Take g = 9.8 m s-2.)

  1. The condition for not slipping On an unbanked road friction alone supplies the centripetal force, so the cyclist does not slip only if v² ≤ µ_s R g.
  2. Convert the speed 18 km/h = 5 m s-1, so v² = 25 m2 s-2.
  3. Compute the limit µ_s R g = 0.1 × 3 × 9.8 = 2.94 m2 s-2.
  4. Compare 25 m2 s-2 is much greater than 2.94 m2 s-2, so the condition is not obeyed.

Answer The cyclist will slip. The speed is too large, or the turn too sharp, for friction to supply the needed centripetal force.

Worked example

A circular racetrack of radius 300 m is banked at an angle of 15°. If the coefficient of friction between the wheels of a race-car and the road is 0.2, what is (a) the optimum speed of the race-car to avoid wear and tear on its tyres, and (b) the maximum permissible speed to avoid slipping? (Take g = 9.8 m s-2.)

  1. (a) Optimum speed At the optimum speed the horizontal component of the normal reaction alone provides the centripetal force and friction is not needed: v_o = √(R g tan θ).
  2. Insert numbers v_o = √(300 × 9.8 × tan 15°) = √(300 × 9.8 × 0.268) ≈ 28.1 m s-1.
  3. (b) Maximum speed Use v_max = √(R g (tan θ + µ_s)/(1 − µ_s tan θ)).
  4. Insert numbers With tan 15° = 0.268 and µ_s = 0.2: (0.268 + 0.2)/(1 − 0.2 × 0.268) = 0.468/0.946 ≈ 0.494. Then v_max = √(300 × 9.8 × 0.494) ≈ 38.1 m s-1.

Answer (a) Optimum speed ≈ 28.1 m s-1. (b) Maximum permissible speed ≈ 38.1 m s-1.

Most real problems involve several bodies pushing and pulling on one another, and every one of them also feels gravity. The working method is always the same: choose any part of the assembly as your system and apply the laws of motion to it, provided you include all forces on that part due to the remaining parts of the assembly.

Step by step: (i) draw a diagram of the assembly, showing the links and supports; (ii) choose a convenient part of the assembly as the system; (iii) draw a separate diagram of that system showing all the forces on it by the rest of the assembly and by other agencies, but not the forces it exerts on the environment. A diagram of this type is called a free-body diagram - and it does not imply that the system has no net force.

(iv) On the free-body diagram, write in the forces whose magnitudes and directions are given or are certain, such as the direction of a tension along its length; treat the rest as unknowns to be found from the laws of motion. (v) If you need a second system, repeat the procedure and use the third law: if the force on A due to B is F in A's diagram, the force on B due to A is −F in B's diagram.

Worked example

A wooden block of mass 2 kg rests on a soft horizontal floor. When an iron cylinder of mass 25 kg is placed on top of the block, the floor yields steadily and the block and the cylinder together go down with an acceleration of 0.1 m s-2. What is the action of the block on the floor (a) before and (b) after the floor yields? Take g = 10 m s-2. Identify the action-reaction pairs in the problem.

  1. (a) Before: free-body diagram of the block Two forces act on the block: gravity 2 × 10 = 20 N downward, and the normal force R of the floor upward. The block is at rest, so by the first law R = 20 N.
  2. (a) Third law The action of the block on the floor is 20 N vertically downward.
  3. (b) Choose the system block + cylinder Its mass is 27 kg, so its weight is 270 N. The free-body diagram shows the weight 270 N downward and the normal force R′ of the floor upward; the internal forces between block and cylinder are not shown.
  4. (b) Second law The system accelerates downward at 0.1 m s-2, so 270 − R′ = 27 × 0.1 = 2.7 N, giving R′ = 267.3 N.
  5. (b) Third law and the pairs The action of the system on the floor is 267.3 N vertically downward. The pairs are: the pull of the earth on the block (or system) and the pull of the block (or system) on the earth; the push of the block (or system) on the floor and the push of the floor on the block (or system); and, after the floor yields, the force of the cylinder on the block and the force of the block on the cylinder.

Answer (a) 20 N vertically downward; (b) 267.3 N vertically downward. The weight and the normal force are not an action-reaction pair: they act on the same body, and after the floor yields they are not even equal (270 N and 267.3 N).

A man of mass 70 kg stands on a weighing scale in a lift that is moving downwards with a uniform acceleration of 5 m s-2. What is the reading on the scale? (Take g = 10 m s-2.) Give the answer in newtons.

Why?

The scale reads the normal force N it exerts on the man. Taking downward as positive, the net downward force is mg − N = m a, so N = m(g − a) = 70 × (10 − 5) = 350 N. If the lift fell freely under gravity, a would equal g and the reading would be zero.

A stone tied to a string is whirled round in a horizontal circle. The speed is increased beyond the maximum the string can bear, and the string breaks suddenly. Which describes the path of the stone from that instant?

Why?

The tension of the string was the force changing the direction of the stone's momentum. The moment the string breaks, no horizontal force acts (neglecting air resistance), so by the first law the stone continues with the velocity it had at that instant - which is along the tangent - in a straight line.

Why can a horse not pull a cart and run in empty space?

Why?

The forward force on the horse-and-cart system must come from outside that system. On the ground it is the friction between the horse's hooves and the ground: the horse pushes the ground backward, the ground pushes it forward. Without ground there is no such external force, and the mutual forces inside the system cannot accelerate it.

Some mechanics problems give you a graph of position against time instead of a list of forces. You can still find the force, and the impulse, because everything follows from what the graph's slope is doing.

For a particle, the velocity is the rate of change of position with time, v = dy/dt (take y as the position coordinate). On a position-time graph, this is the slope of the graph. The acceleration is the rate of change of velocity, a = dv/dt, that is, how fast the slope itself is changing.

Read the graph in three steps. If a stretch of the graph is a straight line, the velocity is constant there, so the acceleration is zero and, by the second law, the force on the particle during that interval is zero. If the slope changes steadily, the velocity is changing, so there is an acceleration and a force F = m a. If the slope changes abruptly at one instant - a kink in the graph - the velocity changes by a definite amount in almost no time, so the momentum changes abruptly: that change in momentum is the impulse delivered at that instant.

The mass comes in twice: to turn the acceleration into a force, F = m a, and to turn a change of velocity into an impulse, impulse = change in momentum = m times the change in velocity. The force at any instant is fixed by the situation at that instant; a particle carries no memory of its earlier motion.

Worked example

A stone of mass 0.25 kg tied to the end of a string is whirled round in a circle of radius 1.5 m with a speed of 40 rev./min in a horizontal plane. What is the tension in the string? What is the maximum speed with which the stone can be whirled around if the string can withstand a maximum tension of 200 N?

  1. Who supplies the centripetal force? The stone moves in a circle at uniform speed, so it needs a force m v^2/R directed towards the centre. The only force acting towards the centre here is the tension in the string, so the tension is the centripetal force: T = m v^2/R.
  2. Convert the speed to m s-1 In one revolution the stone travels the circumference 2 pi R = 2 pi times 1.5, about 9.42 m. At 40 revolutions per minute it makes 40/60 of a revolution each second, so v = 9.42 times 40/60, about 6.28 m s-1.
  3. Tension in the string T = m v^2/R = 0.25 times (6.28)^2 / 1.5, which is about 6.6 N.
  4. Maximum speed from the maximum tension The string can give at most 200 N, so m v^2/R = 200 gives v = square root of (200 times 1.5 / 0.25) = square root of 1200, about 34.6 m s-1.

Answer The tension is about 6.6 N. The maximum speed with which the stone can be whirled is about 34.6 m s-1; beyond that the string must supply more than 200 N and it breaks.

Worked example

A pebble of mass 0.05 kg is thrown vertically upwards. Ignoring air resistance, give the magnitude and direction of the net force on the pebble (a) during its upward motion, (b) during its downward motion, and (c) at the highest point where it is momentarily at rest. (Take g = 10 m s-2.)

  1. What forces act? Air resistance is neglected, so the only force on the pebble at any instant is its weight, mg, directed vertically downward. The earth is still pulling it whether it goes up, comes down, or hangs still for an instant.
  2. Compute the weight mg = 0.05 times 10 = 0.5 N, downward.
  3. The highest point At the highest point the velocity is momentarily zero, but that does not make the force zero. Force and acceleration are fixed by the situation at the location of the body at that instant, not by how fast it happens to be moving: the force is still its weight mg and the acceleration is still g, downward.

Answer (a) 0.5 N downward, (b) 0.5 N downward, (c) 0.5 N downward. The answer is the same in all three cases: a body momentarily at rest can still have a non-zero force and a non-zero acceleration.

Why is it easier to pull a lawn mower than to push it? The answer comes from the two things you already know: the normal reaction and friction.

When two surfaces are in contact, the contact force has a component normal to the surfaces, the normal reaction N, and a component parallel to them, friction. The laws of friction say that sliding friction is f_k = mu_k N (and static friction can rise at most to mu_s N). So the friction you must fight is proportional to the normal reaction: make N smaller and friction becomes smaller too.

Pull the mower by a handle that slants upward, and your pull has an upward component. That upward component partly supports the mower, so the floor has to push up less: the normal reaction N becomes smaller, and with it the friction the mower has to overcome. Push the same mower with a handle slanting downward, and your push has a downward component that presses the mower harder into the ground: N becomes larger, and the friction is larger too. Same mower, same ground - only the direction of your force at the handle has changed, and with it the normal reaction and the friction.

A particle of mass 4 kg has a position-time graph that is a straight line from t = 0 to t = 4 s, and a straight line of a different slope after t = 4 s. What is the force on the particle during the interval from t = 0 to t = 4 s?

Why?

A straight position-time graph means the velocity is not changing, so the acceleration is zero and by the second law the net force is zero. The force at any instant depends only on that instant, not on what happens after t = 4 s. At t = 4 s the slope changes abruptly, so the velocity changes suddenly and an impulse equal to that change in momentum is delivered there.

A stone of mass 0.25 kg tied to a string of length 1.5 m is whirled round in a horizontal circle. If the string can withstand a maximum tension of 200 N, what is the maximum speed with which the stone can be whirled? Give the answer in m s-1, to one decimal place.

Why?

The tension is the centripetal force, so m v^2/R = T gives v = square root of (T R / m) = square root of (200 times 1.5 / 0.25) = square root of 1200, about 34.6 m s-1. Beyond this speed the required centripetal force would exceed 200 N and the string would break.

A pebble of mass 0.05 kg is thrown vertically upwards and, at the highest point of its path, is momentarily at rest. Ignoring air resistance and taking g = 10 m s-2, what is the magnitude of the net force on it at that instant, in newtons?

Why?

The only force is the weight mg = 0.05 times 10 = 0.5 N, directed downward, and it is the same at the highest point as anywhere else. Being momentarily at rest makes the velocity zero, not the force: the acceleration there is g, downward.

Why is it easier to pull a lawn mower than to push it?

Why?

Friction is the component of the contact force parallel to the surfaces, and its magnitude is proportional to the normal reaction N. An upward-slanting pull partly supports the mower, lowering N and hence friction; a downward-slanting push presses the mower into the ground, raising N and hence friction.

Glossary

Inertia
A body's resistance to any change in its state of rest or uniform motion.
Momentum
Mass times velocity, p = m v; a vector pointing along the velocity.
Impulse
Force multiplied by the time it acts; it equals the change in momentum.
Equilibrium
The state of a particle when the net external force on it is zero.
Normal reaction
The part of a contact force that is perpendicular to the surfaces in contact.
Static friction
Self-adjusting friction that keeps a body at rest, up to the limit µ_s N.
Kinetic friction
Friction that opposes actual sliding, equal to µ_k N.
Centripetal force
The name for whatever real force points towards the centre and provides the acceleration v²/R.
Free-body diagram
A diagram of one chosen system showing only the forces acting on it from outside.
Optimum speed (banked road)
The speed √(R g tan θ) at which no friction is needed on a banked turn.

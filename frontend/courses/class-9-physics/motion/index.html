Motion

After this lesson you can:

  • After this lesson you can describe a force by its magnitude, direction and unit, and measure a force with a spring balance.
  • After this lesson you can work out the net force on an object when several forces act on it, and say whether the forces are balanced.
  • After this lesson you can state and apply Newton's three laws of motion, including F = ma and the definition of the newton.
  • After this lesson you can find the acceleration of two connected objects treated as a single system.

A ball lying on the ground starts moving when you kick it. A cricket ball coming towards you changes direction when the bat hits it. A lemon changes shape when your fingers squeeze it. All three changes happen because of a force.

So a force can make an object move from rest, change the speed and direction of motion of a moving object, and change the shape of an object.

Notice that every force in these examples has a direction. Friction acts opposite to motion, magnets push or pull along a particular line, the Earth pulls an object towards itself, and a liquid pushes a floating object upwards with a buoyant force.

Force is therefore a physical quantity for which we must state the direction along with the magnitude and the unit. The SI unit of force is the newton, written with a small 'n' in words, and its symbol is N. The magnitude of a force expresses its strength. If the magnitude, the direction, or both, change, the effect of the force also changes.

How do we measure how large a force is? A spring balance does the job. You have used one to measure the weight of an object, and the weight of an object is the gravitational force with which the Earth pulls it.

A spring balance does more than measure weight. If you pull on its free end, it measures the force with which you pull on the spring inside the balance. So the same instrument measures the magnitude of a force in general.

A light touch is a force of the order of millinewtons, that is 10⁻³ N, about the smallest force a person can directly feel. In specialised experiments, scientists can measure forces as small as yoctonewtons, that is 10⁻²⁴ N.

Which statement about force is correct?

Why?

Force is a physical quantity with a magnitude and a direction, and its SI unit is the newton (N). If the magnitude, the direction, or both change, the effect of the force changes.

In real life, more than one force usually acts on an object at the same time. When you push a box along the floor, your push is one force and the force of friction is another, acting in the opposite direction. When a ball floats on water, the gravitational force of the Earth pulls it down and the buoyant force of the liquid pushes it up.

Think of a tug of war. If both teams pull with equal force, the rope does not move. Two forces which are equal in magnitude but opposite in direction are called balanced forces. If one team pulls harder, the forces are no longer balanced and the rope moves in the direction of the larger force.

When the forces on an object are not balanced, a non-zero net force acts on it. If two opposite forces are unequal, the net force is the difference of their magnitudes and points along the larger force. If two forces act in the same direction, the net force is the sum of their magnitudes and points in that same direction.

Remember the key idea: many forces may act on an object, but its motion depends only on the net force.

Worked example

Two forces of 10 N and 6 N are acting on a block lying on the table. What is the magnitude and the direction of the net force acting on the block in each case? (a) both forces act towards the right; (b) the 10 N force acts towards the right and the 6 N force acts towards the left; (c) the 10 N force acts towards the left and the 6 N force acts towards the right.

  1. (a) Forces in the same direction The two forces act towards the right, so add them: net force = 10 N + 6 N = 16 N, acting towards the right side.
  2. (b) Opposite forces, larger force to the right The forces oppose each other, so subtract: net force = 10 N − 6 N = 4 N, acting towards the right side, that is along the larger force.
  3. (c) Opposite forces, larger force to the left Again subtract: net force = 10 N − 6 N = 4 N, but now acting towards the left side, along the 10 N force.

Answer (a) 16 N towards the right; (b) 4 N towards the right; (c) 4 N towards the left.

A weightlifter lifts a barbell and keeps it steady above her head. Which two forces act on the barbell, and are they balanced?

Why?

The barbell is steady, so its velocity does not change. That needs zero net force, so the upward force of the weightlifter must balance the downward weight.

Two players R and S are in an arm-wrestling match. At the instant when the arms tilt to the front direction (out of the page, towards you), are the forces exerted by the players balanced? If not, which player exerted the larger force?

Why?

When two opposite forces are unequal, the net force points along the larger force, and the arms move in that direction. The tilt towards one side shows whose force is larger.

Keep an object at rest on the floor and push it gently. Often it does not move; you have to push harder. The reason is the force of friction between the bottom surface of the object and the floor, acting opposite to your push.

The box starts moving only when your force is larger than the force of friction, so that a net force acts on the box in the direction of motion.

Two more forces act on a pushed box. The gravitational force, that is its weight, acts downwards. The force exerted by the surface on which the box rests is called the normal force, and it acts upwards, perpendicular to the surface. For a box on a level floor these two forces are balanced. Air also exerts a friction force on a moving box, but usually it is so small that it can be neglected.

Now stop pushing. The box slows down and comes to rest. A bicycle you stop pedalling also comes to rest after some distance. This does not mean motion needs a force to continue; it means friction acts against the motion. To keep an object moving you must keep applying a force that counters friction.

Two simple experiments show how friction behaves on different surfaces. In the first, a stack of four coins is pulled back with a stretched rubber band and released on level surfaces: a wooden table top, a laminated table top, and a polished marble or tiled floor. The rubber band is stretched by the same amount every time.

Before release, the coins are stationary, so the forces on them are balanced. On release, the rubber band pushes the coins forward with a force larger than friction, so a net force acts and the coins accelerate. The moment the coins lose contact with the rubber band, only friction acts on them, opposite to their motion, and it gradually brings them to rest.

The coins travel different distances on different surfaces, so the friction is different. A spring balance pulled horizontally with a wooden block attached gives an approximate measure of the friction: the reading at which the block just starts moving shows the magnitude of the force of friction.

The conclusion is simple. Where friction is smaller, the velocity decreases more slowly and the object travels a larger distance before stopping. Where friction is larger, the object stops sooner.

A thought experiment: what if friction were zero?

Set the friction to zero

Imagine an object and a horizontal floor so smooth that the force of friction between them is zero. A thought experiment is used when the conditions needed for an experiment are difficult to recreate in the real world.

Release the object

Repeat the rubber band experiment on this floor. Once the object loses contact with the rubber band, no force pushes it forward and no friction acts against it.

Nothing can reduce the velocity

With no net force acting on the moving object, its velocity cannot change. It does not slow down at all, so it never comes to rest; it keeps moving forever.

The historical step

Galileo Galilei argued in the 17th century, through a series of thought experiments, that if a body moves along a horizontal plane and all impediments to its motion are removed, it will continue to move indefinitely.

The name for this tendency

Isaac Newton used the word inertia to describe the tendency of objects to resist change in their state of rest or uniform motion, and used this idea to frame his first law of motion.

Using a horizontal force F, a table is moved across the floor at a constant velocity. How much is the frictional force exerted by the floor on the table?

Why?

Constant velocity means the velocity is not changing, so the acceleration and the net force are zero. The friction force must therefore balance the applied force F exactly.

Newton's first law of motion states: an object at rest remains at rest, and an object in motion continues to move with a constant velocity, unless a net force acts upon the object.

In other words, if the net force acting on an object is zero, the object cannot begin to move and cannot change its velocity. Its acceleration is zero.

An object at rest has zero velocity. Constant velocity means there is no change in the magnitude or the direction of the velocity. If that constant velocity is non-zero, the object moves in a straight line in the same direction with the same speed.

The law also settles a common doubt. Push a moving box forward with a force exactly equal to the friction force: the two forces balance, the net force is zero, and the box keeps moving with constant velocity instead of coming to rest.

Worked example

A person is exerting a force on a moving box in the forward direction which is equal to the force of friction acting between the bottom surface of the box and the floor. Will the box continue moving or will it come to rest after some time?

  1. Identify the two forces The person's force acts forward. The force of friction acts backward, opposite to the motion, with the same magnitude.
  2. Find the net force The two forces are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, so they balance each other. The net force on the box is zero.
  3. Apply Newton's first law With zero net force the velocity of the box cannot change, so the box keeps the velocity it already has.

Answer The box will continue moving with constant velocity; it will not come to rest.

An object is moving with a constant velocity. Is there a net force acting upon it?

Why?

Constant velocity means neither the magnitude nor the direction of the velocity is changing, so the acceleration is zero; by Newton's first law the net force must be zero.

Suppose no net force is acting on an object. Which of the following situations is NOT possible?

Why?

With zero net force the acceleration is zero, so the velocity cannot change at all. A constant non-zero acceleration would need a net force.

A ball moves on a smooth frictionless surface. Choose the option that makes all three statements physically correct. (i) If no net force is applied on the ball, the velocity of the ball will ___. (ii) If a net force is applied in the direction of its motion, the magnitude of the velocity will ___. (iii) If a net force is applied opposite to its motion, the magnitude of the velocity will ___.

Why?

No net force leaves the velocity unchanged. A force along the motion accelerates the ball, so its speed increases. A force opposite to the motion accelerates it in the opposite direction, so its speed decreases.

Push a ball gently and it starts moving slowly. Push it hard and it starts moving fast. From this everyday experience you can make a hypothesis: for the same object, a larger force produces a larger acceleration.

To test it, forces of different magnitudes are applied to the same cart on the same surface by hanging weights of different magnitudes from a thread over a pulley, and the time taken to cover a fixed distance is measured. When the force is increased for the same mass, the acceleration increases. So the acceleration of an object of fixed mass increases as the net force on it increases.

Everyday experience gives a second hypothesis: with the same force it is easier to set a lighter object in motion than a heavier one. Repeating the experiment with the same pulling force but a heavier cart shows that the acceleration decreases when the mass increases. So for a given force, the acceleration is inversely related to the mass of the object.

Newton's second law of motion states: when a net force acts on an object, the object accelerates in the direction of the net force. The magnitude of the acceleration is proportional to the magnitude of the net force and is inversely proportional to the mass of the object.

From the second law to F = ma, and the definition of the newton

Write the proportionality

The law says the acceleration is proportional to the net force and inversely proportional to the mass. Combining both statements gives a = F/m.

Rearrange it

Multiplying both sides by m gives F = ma, where F is the net force, m the mass and a the acceleration. The direction of the acceleration is the same as the direction of the net force.

Fix the unit

Mass is measured in kg and acceleration in m s⁻². If m = 1 kg and a = 1 m s⁻², then F = (1 kg) × (1 m s⁻²) = 1 kg m s⁻² = 1 N.

State the definition

One newton of force is the force that produces an acceleration of 1 m s⁻² on an object of mass 1 kg.

Write the weight

Under the gravitational force of the Earth, a falling object has the acceleration g. Using F = ma with a = g gives the gravitational force on an object of mass m as F = mg. The value of g is 9.8 m s⁻², nearly constant near the surface of the Earth; for quick estimations you may take g = 10 m s⁻². Note that g does not depend on the mass of the object.

Worked example

A weightlifter is holding a barbell with a mass of 10 kg fixed on each side of the bar. The mass of the bar itself is 10 kg. How much force is she applying to keep the barbell steady? (Take g = 9.8 m s⁻².)

  1. Total mass of the barbell 10 kg on one side + 10 kg on the other side + 10 kg for the bar = 30 kg.
  2. Weight of the barbell F = mg = 30 kg × 9.8 m s⁻² = 294 N, acting downwards.
  3. Force applied by the weightlifter To keep the barbell steady its velocity must not change, so the net force is zero. She must apply an equal force of 294 N in the opposite direction, that is upwards.

Answer She applies 294 N in the upward direction.

Worked example

A student is trying to push a stationary block of 25 kg on a horizontal floor. The maximum force of friction opposing this motion is 50 N. Determine the displacement of the block in 2 seconds if the student pushes it with a constant force of (i) 50 N and (ii) 55 N in the forward direction.

  1. (i) Applied force equal to the friction The applied force of 50 N equals the opposing force of friction of 50 N. The two forces are balanced and the net force on the block is zero, so the block remains stationary and its displacement is 0 m.
  2. (ii) Net force The net force on the block is 55 N − 50 N = 5 N, in the forward direction.
  3. Acceleration Using Newton's second law, a = F/m = 5 N / 25 kg = 0.2 m s⁻², in the forward direction.
  4. Displacement in 2 s The block starts from rest, so u = 0 m s⁻¹. Using s = ut + ½at², s = 0 × 2 s + ½ × 0.2 m s⁻² × (2 s)² = 0.4 m.

Answer (i) 0 m — the block remains stationary; (ii) 0.4 m in the forward direction.

Worked example

A sports car of mass 1500 kg is moving towards the east. Its velocity–time graph rises as a straight line from 0 m s⁻¹ at t = 0 s to 10 m s⁻¹ at t = 5 s, stays as a straight line parallel to the time axis from 5 s to 10 s at 10 m s⁻¹, and then falls as a straight line from 10 m s⁻¹ at t = 10 s to 0 m s⁻¹ at t = 15 s. Calculate the force acting on the car during (i) 0 s to 5 s, (ii) 5 s to 10 s, and (iii) 10 s to 15 s.

  1. (i) 0 s to 5 s The graph is a straight line inclined to the time axis, so the acceleration is constant, with u = 0 m s⁻¹, v = 10 m s⁻¹ and t = 5 s. From v = u + at: 10 = 0 + a × 5, so a = 2 m s⁻². Then F = ma = 1500 kg × 2 m s⁻² = 3000 N, acting towards the east.
  2. (ii) 5 s to 10 s The graph is parallel to the time axis, so the velocity is constant and the acceleration is zero. Hence no force acts on the car during this interval.
  3. (iii) 10 s to 15 s Here u = 10 m s⁻¹, v = 0 m s⁻¹ and t = 5 s. From v = u + at: 0 = 10 + a × 5, so a = −2 m s⁻². Then F = ma = 1500 kg × (−2 m s⁻²) = −3000 N. The negative sign shows that the force acts opposite to the direction of motion, that is towards the west.

Answer (i) 3000 N towards the east; (ii) zero force; (iii) 3000 N towards the west.

A toy car of mass 100 g is moving with a constant velocity of 0.5 m s⁻¹. What is the net force acting on the toy car?

Why?

Constant velocity means the acceleration is zero. Using F = ma, the net force is therefore zero, whatever the mass of the car.

Two children of different masses are sitting on identical swings. To impart identical initial acceleration, for which child would you require to apply a larger force?

Why?

For the same acceleration a, the force needed is F = ma, which grows with the mass. The heavier child therefore needs the larger force.

How are glass items packed for transportation using a bubble wrap or hay protected from damage?

Why?

A soft packing lengthens the stopping time for the same change of velocity, which means a smaller acceleration; F = ma then gives a smaller force on the glass.

At least two objects must interact for a force to come into play. When you push a ball with your foot, you feel a force applied by the ball on your foot.

Newton's third law of motion states: whenever one object is exerting a force on a second object, the second object is simultaneously exerting an equal and opposite force on the first object.

Sit on a chair with wheels and push a heavy table away from you: your chair moves backwards. Pull the table towards you: your chair moves towards the table. Each time the table applies a force on you in the direction opposite to your push or pull. Two identical spring balances hooked together and pulled apart read the same value every time, showing that the forces they apply on each other are equal in magnitude.

The forces always occur in pairs, but they act on two different objects, so they do not cancel each other. Two equal and opposite forces acting on the same object do balance each other. The law holds for contact and non-contact forces alike: magnetic forces between two magnets, electrostatic forces between two charged balloons, and gravitational forces between the Earth and a fruit all come in equal and opposite pairs.

Worked example

The Earth and a fruit apply equal and opposite gravitational forces on each other. Then why does the fruit move towards the Earth while the Earth does not seem to move towards the fruit?

  1. The forces are equal By Newton's third law, the force on the fruit by the Earth and the force on the Earth by the fruit are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.
  2. But the accelerations are not equal Using a = F/m, the acceleration each body receives depends on its own mass. The mass of the Earth is so large compared with the fruit that the acceleration of the Earth caused by this force is extremely small.
  3. What we notice The acceleration of the fruit is large enough to see, while the effect on the Earth is too small to be noticed.

Answer Both forces are equal in magnitude, but the Earth's enormous mass makes its acceleration extremely small, so its motion is not noticeable.

Worked example

When a 0.1 kg bullet is fired from a 5 kg gun with a force of 2 N, the gun recoils. What are the magnitudes of the initial accelerations of the bullet and of the gun?

  1. The recoil force By Newton's third law, the gun experiences a recoil force of 2 N, equal in magnitude to the force on the bullet.
  2. Acceleration of the gun a = F/m = 2 N / 5 kg = 0.4 m s⁻².
  3. Acceleration of the bullet a = F/m = 2 N / 0.1 kg = 20 m s⁻².

Answer The gun's initial acceleration is 0.4 m s⁻² and the bullet's is 20 m s⁻². The pair of forces is equal in magnitude, but the accelerations are not equal because the masses are different.

Why does a fireperson sometimes struggle when holding the pipe issuing water?

Why?

The pipe pushes the water forward; by Newton's third law the water pushes the pipe backwards with an equal force. The fireperson has to supply the opposite force to keep the pipe steady.

A spacecraft is moving in a region of space where the gravitational force acting upon it is negligible. Suggest how it can change its velocity.

Why?

A velocity changes only when a force acts. With negligible gravity, the spacecraft makes its own force by expelling gas; the gas pushes back on the spacecraft with an equal and opposite force.

A sailor jumps out from a small boat to the shore. As the sailor jumps forward, will the boat move? If yes, in which direction and why?

Why?

The jump is a third-law pair: the sailor pushes the boat backwards, and the boat pushes the sailor forwards. The backward force acts on the boat, so it moves backwards.

The three laws were used for a single object, but they also apply to two or more objects connected together.

Take two boxes of masses m₁ and m₂ on a frictionless horizontal surface, connected by a string, with a force F pulling Box 1 to the right. Box 1 pulls Box 2 through the string and, by Newton's third law, Box 2 pulls Box 1 back with an equal and opposite force. This force in the string is called tension T.

You could find the net force on each box separately. A simpler way is to treat the two boxes and the string as one system. Forces inside the system, such as the tension, are internal forces and need not be considered; only forces acting from outside, such as F, matter.

The system then behaves like a single object of mass m₁ + m₂, so its acceleration is a = F/(m₁ + m₂). Besides F, the external forces are the gravitational force (m₁ + m₂)g acting downwards, which is balanced by the normal force (N₁ + N₂) from the ground.

Finding the acceleration of two connected boxes

Count only the outside forces

Treat the two boxes and the string as one system. The tension acts on both boxes inside the system, so it is an internal force; the pulling force F is the only horizontal external force.

Use the second law on the whole system

The mass of the system is m₁ + m₂, so Newton's second law gives F = (m₁ + m₂)a.

Solve for the acceleration

Dividing both sides by (m₁ + m₂) gives a = F/(m₁ + m₂). The system accelerates just like a single object of that total mass.

Check the vertical forces

The downward gravitational force (m₁ + m₂)g and the upward normal force (N₁ + N₂) are equal and opposite, so they do not affect the horizontal acceleration.

Two boxes of masses m₁ and m₂ on a smooth horizontal surface are connected by a string and pulled by a horizontal force F applied to the first box. What is the acceleration of the system?

Why?

Treat the boxes and the string as one system. The tension is internal and cancels, the only external horizontal force is F, and the total mass is m₁ + m₂, so a = F/(m₁ + m₂).

While practising for the snake boat race, 100 oarsmen row a boat together. 95 of them row backwards to propel the boat forward, but by mistake 5 oarsmen row in the opposite direction. If each oarsman applies a horizontal force of 200 N, what is the net force on the snake boat, in newtons? (Ignore drag forces and air friction.) Give only the number.

Why?

95 oarsmen push forward and 5 push backward, so the effective number is 95 − 5 = 90. The net force is 90 × 200 N = 18000 N, in the forward direction.

Worked example

A bullet of mass 50 g moving with a speed of 100 m s⁻¹ enters a heavy stationary wooden block and stops after penetrating a distance of 50 cm. Assume that the bullet undergoes constant acceleration within the block. Estimate the stopping force acting on the bullet.

  1. Write down what you know in SI units The mass is 50 g, that is 0.05 kg. The penetration distance is 50 cm, that is 0.5 m. Also u = 100 m s⁻¹ and v = 0 m s⁻¹. Let the constant acceleration inside the block be a and the time taken be t.
  2. Use the velocity relation From v = u + at: 0 = 100 + a t, so t = −100/a.
  3. Substitute in the displacement relation From s = ut + ½at²: 0.5 = 100t + ½at². Putting t = −100/a gives 0.5 = −10000/a + 5000/a = −5000/a, so a = −10000 m s⁻².
  4. Find the stopping force The magnitude of the force is F = ma = 0.05 kg × 10000 m s⁻² = 500 N, acting opposite to the bullet's motion.

Answer The stopping force is about 500 N, opposite to the direction of motion of the bullet.

Worked example

An object of mass 2 kg moving with a constant velocity of 10 m s⁻¹ encounters a rough patch where the force of friction on the object is 7 N. At the same time, an additional constant force of 3 N opposing the motion is applied on the object. After entering the rough patch, how much distance does the object travel before coming to rest?

  1. Total opposing force Both forces oppose the motion, so they add: 7 N + 3 N = 10 N backwards.
  2. Acceleration a = F/m = 10 N / 2 kg = 5 m s⁻², directed opposite to the motion. Taking the motion as positive, a = −5 m s⁻².
  3. Time taken to stop From v = u + at: 0 = 10 + (−5)t, so t = 2 s.
  4. Distance travelled From s = ut + ½at²: s = 10 × 2 + ½ × (−5) × (2)² = 20 − 10 = 10 m.

Answer The object travels 10 m before coming to rest.

One force, two loads: combining accelerations into a formula

The harrow alone

The tractor pulls mass m₁ with a net force F and produces acceleration a₁, so F = m₁a₁ and therefore m₁ = F/a₁.

The trolley alone

The same force F on mass m₂ produces acceleration a₂, so F = m₂a₂ and therefore m₂ = F/a₂.

Harrow on the trolley

Now the tractor pulls the trolley carrying the harrow with the same force F. Treating them as one system, the total mass is m₁ + m₂ and the acceleration is a = F/(m₁ + m₂).

Substitute and simplify

Put m₁ = F/a₁ and m₂ = F/a₂ into the expression: a = F / (F/a₁ + F/a₂). Cancel F: a = 1 / (1/a₁ + 1/a₂) = a₁a₂ / (a₁ + a₂).

A footballer kicks a football of mass 0.4 kg and gives it a speed of 108 km h⁻¹. The estimated force imparted was 800 N. Calculate the time of contact between the foot and the ball, in seconds. Give only the number.

Why?

First convert the speed: 108 km h⁻¹ = 108 × 1000 m / 3600 s = 30 m s⁻¹. Then a = F/m = 800 N / 0.4 kg = 2000 m s⁻². The ball starts from rest, so from v = u + at: 30 = 0 + 2000t, giving t = 30/2000 = 0.015 s.

When the pole of a bar magnet is brought close to a magnetic compass, the bar magnet and the compass needle exert equal and opposite magnetic forces on each other. Yet the compass needle moves and the bar magnet does not move. Why?

Why?

Equal forces on two different masses give different accelerations, because a = F/m. The needle's small mass gives it a visible acceleration while the magnet's is negligible.

During a high jump event, a landing mat or sand bed is placed for the athlete to fall upon. Why does this reduce the risk of injury?

Why?

For the same change of velocity, a longer stopping time means a smaller acceleration; F = ma then gives a smaller force on the athlete, just as an airbag does in a vehicle.

Glossary

force
A push or pull that can start an object moving, change its speed or direction, or change its shape; it has a magnitude, a direction and the unit newton (N).
balanced forces
Two forces that are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, so that the object does not move.
net force
The single force left after adding all forces: a sum if they are in the same direction, a difference if they are opposite. Only the net force decides the motion.
normal force
The upward push of a surface on an object resting on it, perpendicular to the surface.
inertia
The tendency of an object to resist any change in its state of rest or uniform motion.
tension
The force carried by a string joining two objects; it pulls each object in the opposite direction.
newton
The SI unit of force: the force that gives a 1 kg object an acceleration of 1 m s⁻².

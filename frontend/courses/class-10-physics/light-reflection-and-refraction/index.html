Light – Reflection and Refraction

After this lesson you can:

  • After this lesson you can explain how reflected light lets us see objects and state the laws of reflection.
  • After this lesson you can describe spherical mirrors, their terms, and the images formed by concave and convex mirrors.
  • After this lesson you can use the mirror formula, lens formula, magnification and power of a lens to solve numerical problems with the New Cartesian Sign Convention.
  • After this lesson you can explain refraction through a glass slab, Snell’s law, refractive index and optical density.

We see an object because light falls on it and the object reflects that light. This reflected light, when it reaches our eyes, makes the object visible. A transparent medium also lets light pass through it. In a dark room there is no light to reflect, so we cannot see the objects.

Light seems to travel in straight lines. A small source of light casts a sharp shadow of an opaque object, which points to this straight-line path. We usually show this straight-line path as a ray of light.

When light falls on a polished surface such as a mirror, it is reflected. The laws of reflection are simple. The angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection. The incident ray, the normal at the point of incidence, and the reflected ray all lie in the same plane. These laws apply to all reflecting surfaces, including spherical surfaces.

A plane mirror forms an image that is always virtual and erect. The size of the image is equal to the size of the object. The image is as far behind the mirror as the object is in front of it. The image is also laterally inverted, which means left and right are interchanged.

Which of these describes the image formed by a plane mirror?

Why?

A plane mirror always forms a virtual, erect image. Its size equals the object size, and it lies as far behind the mirror as the object is in front of it. The image is also laterally inverted.

A curved shining surface, such as the curved surface of a spoon, acts like a curved mirror. The most common curved mirror is the spherical mirror, whose reflecting surface forms a part of the surface of a sphere. If the reflecting surface is curved inwards, the mirror is a concave mirror. If it is curved outwards, the mirror is a convex mirror.

The centre of the reflecting surface is the pole P. The centre of the sphere of which the mirror forms a part is the centre of curvature C; it lies outside the mirror, in front of a concave mirror and behind a convex mirror. The radius of that sphere is the radius of curvature R, and distance PC equals R. The straight line through P and C is the principal axis, and it is normal to the mirror at P. The diameter of the reflecting surface is the aperture. We consider mirrors whose aperture is much smaller than their radius of curvature.

Rays parallel to the principal axis meet at a point after reflection from a concave mirror. This point is the principal focus F. In a convex mirror the reflected rays appear to diverge from F. The distance between P and F is the focal length f. For a spherical mirror of small aperture, R = 2f, so F lies midway between P and C.

For a concave mirror, the image depends on the object position. At infinity the image is real, inverted, highly diminished and point-sized at F. Beyond C it is real, inverted and diminished between F and C. At C it is real, inverted and of the same size at C. Between C and F it is real, inverted and enlarged beyond C. At F no image is formed. Between P and F the image is virtual, erect and enlarged behind the mirror.

For a convex mirror, an object at infinity gives a virtual, erect, highly diminished point-sized image at F behind the mirror. An object between infinity and P gives a virtual, erect and diminished image between P and F behind the mirror. Concave mirrors are used in torches, search-lights, vehicle headlights, shaving mirrors, by dentists, and in solar furnaces. Convex mirrors are used as rear-view mirrors because they give an erect, diminished image and a wider field of view.

A spherical mirror has radius of curvature 20 cm. What is its focal length in cm?

Why?

For a spherical mirror of small aperture, R = 2f. So f = R/2 = 20 cm / 2 = 10 cm.

Name a mirror that can give an erect and enlarged image of an object.

Why?

When the object is between the pole and the principal focus of a concave mirror, the image is virtual, erect and enlarged.

Define the principal focus of a concave mirror.

Why?

For a concave mirror, parallel rays converge at the principal focus after reflection.

The image formed by a concave mirror is observed to be virtual, erect and larger than the object. Where should the object be placed?

Why?

For a concave mirror, the virtual, erect, enlarged image occurs when the object lies between P and F.

We wish to obtain an erect image using a concave mirror of focal length 15 cm. What should be the range of object distance and the nature of the image?

Why?

An erect image from a concave mirror is virtual and enlarged, and it is formed when the object is between the pole and the principal focus.

Name the type of mirror used in these situations: (a) headlights of a car, (b) side/rear-view mirror of a vehicle, (c) solar furnace.

Why?

Concave mirrors give powerful parallel beams in headlights and concentrate sunlight in solar furnaces. Convex mirrors give an erect, diminished image and a wider field of view for rear-view use.

Why do we prefer a convex mirror as a rear-view mirror in vehicles?

Why?

A convex mirror always gives an erect, diminished image and, being curved outwards, shows a larger area than a plane mirror.

A ray diagram helps you locate an image without drawing every ray. An extended object can be treated as many point sources, and each point sends out many rays. For clarity, we choose any two rays whose directions after reflection are easy to know. The point where at least two reflected rays meet, or appear to meet, gives the image position.

For a concave mirror, a ray parallel to the principal axis passes through F after reflection. For a convex mirror, the same ray appears to diverge from F. A ray passing through F of a concave mirror, or directed towards F of a convex mirror, emerges parallel to the principal axis after reflection.

A ray passing through C of a concave mirror, or directed towards C of a convex mirror, is reflected back along the same path. This happens because the ray falls along the normal to the reflecting surface. A ray incident obliquely at the pole P is reflected obliquely, following the laws of reflection and making equal angles with the principal axis.

In every case, the laws of reflection are followed at the point of incidence: the angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence. You can use any two of these ray rules to draw the image for a given object position.

A ray passing through the centre of curvature of a concave mirror is reflected how?

Why?

The ray falls along the normal to the reflecting surface at the point of incidence, so it is reflected back along the same path.

To use mirror equations, we follow the New Cartesian Sign Convention. The pole P is the origin, and the principal axis is the x-axis. The object is always placed to the left of the mirror, so light falls on the mirror from the left. Distances measured to the right of P are positive, and distances to the left are negative. Distances measured above the principal axis are positive, and those below are negative.

The object distance is u, the image distance is v, and the focal length is f. The mirror formula is 1/v + 1/u = 1/f. It is valid in all situations for all spherical mirrors and for all object positions. You must put the signs of u, v and f correctly before solving.

Magnification m gives how large the image is compared with the object. It is m = h'/h, where h is the object height and h' is the image height. It is also m = -v/u. A negative value of m indicates a real image, and a positive value indicates a virtual image. The height of the object is taken as positive because it is above the principal axis. The image height is positive for a virtual image and negative for a real image.

Worked example

A convex mirror used for rear-view on an automobile has a radius of curvature of 3.00 m. If a bus is located at 5.00 m from this mirror, find the position, nature and size of the image.

  1. Write the given values with signs R = +3.00 m. Object distance u = –5.00 m. For a convex mirror the principal focus is behind the mirror, so f = R/2 = +3.00 m / 2 = +1.50 m.
  2. Use the mirror formula 1/v + 1/u = 1/f, so 1/v = 1/f – 1/u = 1/1.50 – 1/(–5.00) = 1/1.50 + 1/5.00.
  3. Calculate v 1/v = (5.00 + 1.50) / 7.50 = 6.50 / 7.50, so v = +7.50 / 6.50 = +1.15 m. The image is 1.15 m behind the mirror.
  4. Calculate magnification m = –v/u = –(1.15 m)/(–5.00 m) = +0.23. A positive m means a virtual, erect image, and the size is smaller by a factor of 0.23.

Answer The image is 1.15 m behind the mirror. It is virtual, erect and smaller than the object by a factor of 0.23.

Worked example

An object, 4.0 cm in size, is placed at 25.0 cm in front of a concave mirror of focal length 15.0 cm. At what distance from the mirror should a screen be placed in order to obtain a sharp image? Find the nature and the size of the image.

  1. Write the given values with signs Object height h = +4.0 cm. Object distance u = –25.0 cm. For a concave mirror f = –15.0 cm. We need v and h'.
  2. Use the mirror formula 1/v + 1/u = 1/f, so 1/v = 1/f – 1/u = 1/(–15.0) – 1/(–25.0) = –1/15.0 + 1/25.0.
  3. Calculate v 1/v = (–5.0 + 3.0)/75.0 = –2.0/75.0, so v = –37.5 cm. The screen should be placed 37.5 cm in front of the mirror. The image is real.
  4. Calculate image height m = h'/h = –v/u, so h' = –v h/u = –(–37.5 cm)(+4.0 cm)/(–25.0 cm) = –6.0 cm. The image is inverted and enlarged.

Answer The screen should be placed at 37.5 cm in front of the mirror. The image is real, inverted and 6.0 cm tall, so it is enlarged.

Find the focal length of a convex mirror whose radius of curvature is 32 cm. Give the answer in cm.

Why?

For a spherical mirror of small aperture, R = 2f. So f = R/2 = 32 cm / 2 = 16 cm.

A concave mirror produces three times magnified real image of an object placed 10 cm in front of it. How far is the image from the mirror, in cm?

Why?

A real image has negative magnification. m = –v/u = –3, with u = –10 cm. Thus –v/(–10) = –3, so v = –30 cm. The image is 30 cm in front of the mirror.

An object is placed at a distance of 10 cm from a convex mirror of focal length 15 cm. Find the image distance in cm.

Why?

Use 1/v + 1/u = 1/f with u = –10 cm and f = +15 cm. 1/v = 1/15 + 1/10 = 1/6, so v = +6 cm. The image is 6 cm behind the mirror.

An object of size 7.0 cm is placed at 27 cm in front of a concave mirror of focal length 18 cm. At what distance from the mirror should a screen be placed, in cm, so that a sharp focussed image is obtained?

Why?

u = –27 cm, f = –18 cm. 1/v = 1/f – 1/u = –1/18 + 1/27 = –1/54, so v = –54 cm. The screen is placed 54 cm in front of the mirror.

Light travels in straight lines in a transparent medium, but its direction can change when it enters another transparent medium obliquely. This change of direction is called refraction of light. You see examples of it when the bottom of a pond appears raised, when letters under a glass slab appear raised, when a pencil partly in water appears displaced, and when a lemon in water looks bigger from the sides.

In a rectangular glass slab, the light ray changes direction at two surfaces. At the air-glass surface it passes from a rarer medium to a denser medium and bends towards the normal. At the glass-air surface it passes from a denser medium to a rarer medium and bends away from the normal.

The emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray. This happens because the bending at the two opposite parallel faces is equal and opposite. The ray is shifted sideward slightly, but its direction remains parallel to the original direction.

A pencil partly immersed in water in a glass tumbler appears displaced at the interface. Why?

Why?

The light reaching you from the part of the pencil inside water seems to come from a different direction because of refraction.

What is true about the emergent ray from a rectangular glass slab?

Why?

The bending at the two opposite parallel faces is equal and opposite, so the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray but shifted sideward.

Refraction follows two laws. First, the incident ray, the refracted ray and the normal at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane. Second, the ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is a constant for light of a given colour and for a given pair of media. This is Snell’s law, and it is true for 0 < i < 90°.

This constant is called the refractive index of the second medium with respect to the first. If light travels from medium 1 to medium 2, and its speeds are v1 and v2, then n21 = v1/v2. The refractive index of medium 1 with respect to medium 2 is n12 = v2/v1.

If medium 1 is air or vacuum, the refractive index is called the absolute refractive index. It is n = c/v, where c is the speed of light in air and v is the speed in the medium. Light travels fastest in vacuum with speed 3 × 10^8 m s–1. It is only marginally less in air and reduces considerably in glass or water.

The medium with the larger refractive index is optically denser, and the medium with the lower refractive index is optically rarer. Light travels faster in a rarer medium. A ray going from rarer to denser slows down and bends towards the normal; a ray going from denser to rarer speeds up and bends away from the normal. Optical density is not the same as mass density: kerosene has a higher refractive index than water, so it is optically denser than water, although its mass density is less.

A ray of light travelling in air enters obliquely into water. Does it bend towards the normal or away from the normal, and why?

Why?

Water has a larger refractive index than air, so it is optically denser. A ray from rarer to denser slows down and bends towards the normal.

Light enters from air to glass having refractive index 1.50. The speed of light in vacuum is 3 × 10^8 m/s. What is the speed of light in glass in m/s?

Why?

n = c/v, so v = c/n = (3 × 10^8 m/s)/1.50 = 2 × 10^8 m/s = 200000000 m/s.

From the refractive-index table, which medium has the highest optical density and which has the lowest?

Why?

Diamond has refractive index 2.42, the largest listed, and air has 1.0003, the smallest listed. Larger refractive index means optically denser.

You are given kerosene, turpentine and water. In which of these does light travel fastest?

Why?

Water has the smallest refractive index among the three (1.33, compared with 1.44 for kerosene and 1.47 for turpentine), so light travels fastest in water.

The refractive index of diamond is 2.42. What is the meaning of this statement?

Why?

For absolute refractive index, n = c/v. A value 2.42 means c is 2.42 times the speed in diamond.

A lens is a transparent material bound by two surfaces, of which one or both are spherical. A convex lens is thicker at the middle than at the edges, and it converges light rays, so it is a converging lens. A concave lens is thicker at the edges than at the middle, and it diverges light rays, so it is a diverging lens.

A lens has two centres of curvature C1 and C2. The principal axis passes through these centres. The central point of the lens is the optical centre O. A ray passing through O passes without deviation. The effective diameter of the circular outline is the aperture. We consider thin lenses with small apertures.

Rays parallel to the principal axis converge at the principal focus after refraction through a convex lens. In a concave lens they appear to diverge from the principal focus on the same side. A lens has two principal foci F1 and F2, and the focal length f is the distance of the principal focus from the optical centre. The distance between the lens and the sharp image of the Sun gives an approximate focal length.

For a convex lens, the image table is: object at infinity gives a real, inverted, highly diminished point-sized image at F2; beyond 2F1 gives a real, inverted, diminished image between F2 and 2F2; at 2F1 gives a real, inverted same-size image at 2F2; between F1 and 2F1 gives a real, inverted enlarged image beyond 2F2; at F1 gives no image; between F1 and O gives a virtual, erect, enlarged image on the same side as the object.

A concave lens always gives a virtual, erect and diminished image, whatever the object position. For ray diagrams, a ray parallel to the principal axis passes through F after refraction in a convex lens or appears to diverge from F in a concave lens. A ray through F emerges parallel to the principal axis. A ray through the optical centre passes without deviation.

Which one of the following materials cannot be used to make a lens?

Why?

A lens is made of a transparent material. Clay is not transparent, so it cannot be used to make a lens.

Which type of lens would you prefer to use while reading small letters found in a dictionary?

Why?

A convex lens can form a virtual, erect and enlarged image when the object is between the principal focus F1 and the optical centre O. A concave lens always gives a virtual, erect but diminished image, so it cannot enlarge the letters.

Where should an object be placed in front of a convex lens to get a real image of the same size as the object?

Why?

For a convex lens, an object at 2F1 gives a real, inverted image of the same size at 2F2.

A concave lens is used to look at an object. What is the nature of the image formed?

Why?

A concave lens always gives a virtual, erect and diminished image, irrespective of the position of the object.

For lenses we use a sign convention similar to that for mirrors, but all measurements are taken from the optical centre of the lens. The focal length of a convex lens is positive, and the focal length of a concave lens is negative. Take care to put the correct signs for u, v, f, object height h and image height h'.

The lens formula is 1/v - 1/u = 1/f. It is general and valid in all situations for any spherical lens. The magnification of a lens is m = h'/h = v/u.

The power of a lens is P = 1/f. Its SI unit is the dioptre, denoted by D. If f is in metres, power is in dioptres. One dioptre is the power of a lens whose focal length is 1 metre, so 1 D = 1 m–1. The power of a convex lens is positive, and the power of a concave lens is negative.

Many optical instruments use several lenses. For lenses placed in contact, the net power is the algebraic sum of the individual powers: P = P1 + P2 + P3 + … . For example, a lens of power +2.0 D is convex with focal length +0.50 m, and a lens of power –2.5 D is concave with focal length –0.40 m.

Worked example

A concave lens has focal length of 15 cm. At what distance should the object from the lens be placed so that it forms an image at 10 cm from the lens? Also, find the magnification produced by the lens.

  1. Write the given values with signs A concave lens always forms a virtual, erect image on the same side of the object. Image distance v = –10 cm; focal length f = –15 cm; object distance u = ?
  2. Use the lens formula 1/v – 1/u = 1/f, so 1/u = 1/v – 1/f = 1/(–10) – 1/(–15) = –1/10 + 1/15.
  3. Calculate u 1/u = (–3 + 2)/30 = –1/30, so u = –30 cm. The object distance is 30 cm.
  4. Calculate magnification m = v/u = (–10 cm)/(–30 cm) = +1/3 = +0.33. The positive sign shows the image is erect and virtual. The image is one-third of the object size.

Answer The object is 30 cm from the lens. The magnification is +0.33, so the image is virtual, erect and one-third of the object size.

Worked example

A 2.0 cm tall object is placed perpendicular to the principal axis of a convex lens of focal length 10 cm. The distance of the object from the lens is 15 cm. Find the nature, position and size of the image. Also find its magnification.

  1. Write the given values with signs Object height h = +2.0 cm; focal length f = +10 cm; object distance u = –15 cm; image distance v = ?; image height h' = ?
  2. Use the lens formula 1/v – 1/u = 1/f, so 1/v = 1/f + 1/u = 1/10 + 1/(–15) = 1/10 – 1/15.
  3. Calculate v 1/v = (3 – 2)/30 = 1/30, so v = +30 cm. The image is 30 cm on the other side of the optical centre. The image is real and inverted.
  4. Calculate image height and magnification m = h'/h = v/u, so h' = h(v/u) = 2.0 × (+30/–15) = –4.0 cm. Also m = v/u = +30/–15 = –2. The negative signs show the image is inverted and real.

Answer The image is formed 30 cm on the other side of the lens. It is real, inverted and 4.0 cm tall, so it is two times enlarged.

Worked example

An object 5 cm in length is held 25 cm away from a converging lens of focal length 10 cm. Find the position, size and the nature of the image formed.

  1. Write the given values with signs Object height h = +5 cm; object distance u = –25 cm; focal length f = +10 cm for a converging (convex) lens. We need v and h'.
  2. Use the lens formula 1/v – 1/u = 1/f, so 1/v = 1/f + 1/u = 1/10 + 1/(–25) = 1/10 – 1/25.
  3. Calculate v 1/v = (5 – 2)/50 = 3/50, so v = +50/3 cm ≈ 16.7 cm. The image is on the other side of the lens and is real.
  4. Calculate image height m = v/u = (50/3)/(–25) = –2/3. Then h' = h × m = 5 × (–2/3) ≈ –3.3 cm. The image is inverted and diminished.

Answer The image is about 16.7 cm from the lens on the other side. It is real, inverted and about 3.3 cm tall, so it is diminished.

A concave lens of focal length 15 cm forms an image 10 cm from the lens. How far is the object placed from the lens, in cm?

Why?

Use 1/v – 1/u = 1/f with v = –10 cm and f = –15 cm. 1/u = 1/v – 1/f = –1/10 + 1/15 = –1/30, so u = –30 cm. The object is 30 cm from the lens.

Find the focal length and type of a lens of power –2.0 D.

Why?

P = 1/f, so f = 1/P = 1/(–2.0) m = –0.50 m. A negative power means a concave lens.

A doctor has prescribed a corrective lens of power +1.5 D. What is its focal length and type?

Why?

f = 1/P = 1/1.5 m ≈ +0.67 m. Positive power means a convex, converging lens.

Define 1 dioptre of power of a lens.

Why?

The SI unit of power is the dioptre, and 1 D = 1 m–1.

Find the power of a concave lens of focal length 2 m, in dioptres.

Why?

P = 1/f = 1/(–2 m) = –0.5 D. The negative sign shows a concave lens.

A convex lens forms a real and inverted image of a needle at a distance of 50 cm from it. The image is equal to the size of the object. Where is the needle placed, and what is the power of the lens?

Why?

A convex lens gives a real, same-size image when the object is at 2F and the image is at 2F. Since v = 50 cm, f = 25 cm = 0.25 m. The object is also at 50 cm. Power P = 1/f = 1/0.25 = +4 D.

The magnification produced by a plane mirror is +1. What does this mean?

Why?

Magnification is m = h'/h. A value of +1 means h' equals h, so the image is the same size as the object. A positive value of m indicates a virtual image, and the plane-mirror image is virtual and erect.

Glossary

real image
An image that can be formed on a screen; for mirrors and lenses in this chapter it is inverted when formed by a single mirror or lens in the cases tabulated.
virtual image
An image from which rays appear to come but do not actually meet; it cannot be obtained on a screen in the usual way and is erect in the cases described.
laterally inverted
An image in which left and right are interchanged, as in a plane mirror.
snell’s law
The ratio sin i / sin r is constant for a given colour of light and a given pair of media.
absolute refractive index
The refractive index of a medium with respect to air or vacuum, equal to c/v.
converging lens
A convex lens, thicker at the middle, which makes parallel rays converge.
diverging lens
A concave lens, thicker at the edges, which makes parallel rays diverge.
dioptre
The SI unit of power of a lens; 1 D = 1 m–1.
thin lens
A lens whose aperture is much smaller than its radius of curvature and whose two centres of curvature are equidistant from the optical centre.
principal focus of a lens
The point on the principal axis where rays parallel to the principal axis meet, or appear to come from, after refraction through the lens.

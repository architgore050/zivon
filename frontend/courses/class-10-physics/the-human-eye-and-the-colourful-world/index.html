Of all our sense organs, the eye is the one that lets us see the world and its colours. Close your eyes and you can still recognise many things by smell, taste, sound or touch, but you cannot tell what colour anything is.

The human eye works very much like a camera. Its lens system forms an image on a light-sensitive screen inside the eye, and that screen is a membrane called the retina.

The eyeball is roughly spherical, with a diameter of about 2.3 cm. Light enters it through a thin membrane at the front called the cornea, which forms a transparent bulge on the front surface of the eyeball.

Most of the refraction of the light entering the eye happens at the outer surface of the cornea. The crystalline lens inside the eye only makes the finer adjustment of focal length needed to focus objects at different distances on the retina.

Behind the cornea sits the iris, a dark muscular diaphragm. It controls the size of the pupil, the opening at its centre, and the pupil regulates and controls the amount of light entering the eye.

At the back of the eye, the retina is a delicate membrane packed with an enormous number of light-sensitive cells. When light falls on them they get activated and generate electrical signals.

These signals travel along the optic nerves to the brain. The brain interprets them and processes the information, so that we perceive objects as they are.

The eye lens forms an inverted real image of the object on the retina. It is the brain that makes sense of this image for us.

Where does most of the refraction of light entering the human eye take place?

Why?

Most of the refraction happens at the outer surface of the cornea. The crystalline lens only provides the finer adjustment of focal length needed to focus objects at different distances on the retina.

The eye lens is made of a fibrous, jelly-like material. Its curvature can be changed to some extent by the ciliary muscles, and changing the curvature changes the focal length of the lens.

When the ciliary muscles are relaxed, the lens becomes thin. Its focal length increases, and this lets us see distant objects clearly.

When you look at an object closer to the eye, the ciliary muscles contract. The curvature of the eye lens increases, the lens becomes thicker, and its focal length decreases. This lets us see nearby objects clearly.

The ability of the eye lens to adjust its focal length is called accommodation.

There is a limit to accommodation. The focal length of the eye lens cannot be decreased below a certain minimum. Try reading a printed page held very close to your eyes: the image blurs and the eye feels strained.

To see an object comfortably and distinctly, hold it about 25 cm from the eyes. The minimum distance at which objects can be seen most distinctly without strain is called the least distance of distinct vision, or the near point of the eye. For a young adult with normal vision the near point is about 25 cm.

The farthest point up to which the eye can see objects clearly is called the far point of the eye. For a normal eye the far point is infinity. So a normal eye can see clearly objects that are anywhere between 25 cm and infinity.

Sometimes the crystalline lens of people at old age becomes milky and cloudy. This condition is called cataract. It causes partial or complete loss of vision, and vision can be restored through a cataract surgery.

Why?

What happens to the image distance in the eye when we increase the distance of an object from the eye?

Why?

The retina is fixed at the back of the eye, so the image must always fall on it. When the object moves farther away, the eye does not move the image; instead the ciliary muscles change the curvature of the eye lens and hence its focal length, so that the image still lands on the retina.

Sometimes the eye gradually loses its power of accommodation. The person then cannot see objects distinctly and comfortably, and vision becomes blurred because of refractive defects of the eye.

There are mainly three common refractive defects of vision: myopia or near-sightedness, hypermetropia or far-sightedness, and presbyopia. They can be corrected by using suitable spherical lenses.

Myopia is also known as near-sightedness. A person with myopia can see nearby objects clearly but cannot see distant objects distinctly. The far point of such a person is nearer than infinity; the person may see clearly only up to a distance of a few metres.

In a myopic eye, the image of a distant object is formed in front of the retina and not at the retina itself. This defect may arise because of excessive curvature of the eye lens, or because the eyeball has become elongated.

Myopia is corrected by using a concave lens of suitable power. The concave lens brings the image back on to the retina.

Hypermetropia is also known as far-sightedness. A person with hypermetropia can see distant objects clearly but cannot see nearby objects distinctly. The near point of such a person is farther away than the normal 25 cm, so the person has to keep reading material much beyond 25 cm from the eye.

This happens because light rays from a close by object are focussed at a point behind the retina. The defect arises either because the focal length of the eye lens is too long, or because the eyeball has become too small.

Hypermetropia is corrected by using a convex lens of appropriate power. Eye-glasses with converging lenses provide the additional focussing power needed to form the image on the retina.

The power of accommodation of the eye usually decreases with ageing, and the near point gradually recedes. People then find it difficult to see nearby objects comfortably and distinctly without corrective eye-glasses. This defect is called presbyopia. It arises due to the gradual weakening of the ciliary muscles and diminishing flexibility of the eye lens.

Sometimes a person may suffer from both myopia and hypermetropia. Such people often require bi-focal lenses, which consist of both concave and convex lenses: the upper portion is a concave lens for distant vision, and the lower part is a convex lens for near vision.

These days refractive defects can also be corrected with contact lenses or through surgical interventions.

Lens power and focal length

Power is measured in dioptres

Lens power is quoted in dioptres, written D. The numerical problems in this chapter use values such as -5.5 D for distant vision and +1.5 D for near vision.

Power and focal length

The power P of a lens and its focal length f in metres are related by P = 1/f, so f = 1/P. Work with f in metres and the power comes out in dioptres.

The sign of the power tells you which lens

The lens used to correct myopia, which is a concave lens, is the one the chapter quotes with a minus sign (-5.5 D). The lens used to correct hypermetropia, which is a convex lens, is the one quoted with a plus sign (+1.5 D).

  2. Choose the relation Power and focal length are related by P = 1/f with f in metres, so f = 1/P. The answer comes out in metres.
  3. Distant vision f = 1/(-5.5) = -0.18 m, that is about -18 cm. The negative sign shows it is a concave lens.
  4. Near vision f = 1/(+1.5) = +0.67 m, that is about +67 cm. The positive sign shows it is a convex lens.

Light passing through a rectangular glass slab comes out parallel to the direction in which it entered, only slightly displaced sideways, because the slab's two refracting surfaces are parallel.

A triangular glass prism is different. It has two triangular bases and three rectangular lateral surfaces, and these surfaces are inclined to each other. The angle between its two lateral faces is called the angle of the prism.

When a ray of light PE falls on one refracting face of the prism, it bends towards the normal as it enters the glass and travels inside as the refracted ray EF. At the second face it passes from glass to air, so it bends away from the normal and leaves as the emergent ray FS.

The peculiar shape of the prism makes the emergent ray bend at an angle to the direction of the incident ray. This angle is called the angle of deviation.

When a narrow beam of white light from the Sun is allowed to fall on a glass prism and the emerging light is caught on a nearby screen, a beautiful band of colours appears. The prism has split the white light into a band of colours.

The colours you see, in order, are violet, indigo, blue, green, yellow, orange and red. The acronym VIBGYOR helps you remember the sequence.

The band of the coloured components of a light beam is called its spectrum. The splitting of light into its component colours is called dispersion.

Why do we get these colours? Different colours of light bend through different angles with respect to the incident ray as they pass through a prism. Red light bends the least and violet bends the most. So the rays of each colour emerge along different paths and become distinct.

Isaac Newton was the first to use a glass prism to obtain the spectrum of sunlight. When he tried to split the colours further with another similar prism, he could not get any more colours. When he placed a second identical prism in an inverted position, all the colours passed through it and a beam of white light emerged on the other side. This gave Newton the idea that sunlight is made up of seven colours.

Any light that gives a spectrum similar to that of sunlight is often referred to as white light.

A rainbow is a natural spectrum appearing in the sky after a rain shower. It is caused by dispersion of sunlight by the tiny water droplets present in the atmosphere. A rainbow is always formed in a direction opposite to that of the Sun.

The water droplets act like small prisms. They refract and disperse the incident sunlight, then reflect it internally, and finally refract it again as it comes out of the raindrop. Because of this dispersion and internal reflection, different colours reach the observer's eye.

You can also see a rainbow on a sunny day by looking at the sky through a waterfall or a water fountain with the Sun behind you.

When white light passes through a glass prism, which colour bends the most and which the least?

Why?

Different colours bend through different angles as they pass through the prism. Red light bends the least and violet the most, which is why the colours come out along separate paths and form a spectrum.

You may have seen objects wavering or flickering when you look at them through the hot air rising above a fire or a radiator. The air just above the fire is hotter than the air further up.

Hotter air is lighter, that is, less dense, than the cooler air above it, and its refractive index is slightly less than that of the cooler air. Since the physical conditions of the air are not stationary, the apparent position of the object seen through the hot air keeps fluctuating. This is atmospheric refraction on a small scale in our local environment.

The twinkling of stars is a similar phenomenon on a much larger scale. Starlight, on entering the earth's atmosphere, undergoes refraction continuously before it reaches the earth. The atmosphere bends starlight towards the normal, so the apparent position of the star is slightly different from its actual position.

A star near the horizon appears slightly higher than its actual position. Its apparent position is not stationary but keeps changing slightly, because the physical conditions of the atmosphere are not stationary. As the path of the light rays keeps varying, the apparent position of the star fluctuates and the amount of starlight entering the eye flickers, so the star sometimes appears brighter and at other times fainter. That is the twinkling effect.

The planets do not twinkle. They are much closer to the earth and are seen as extended sources. If we think of a planet as a collection of a large number of point-sized sources of light, the total variation in the amount of light entering our eye from all those sources averages out to zero, which nullifies the twinkling effect.

Because of atmospheric refraction, the Sun is visible to us about 2 minutes before the actual sunrise and about 2 minutes after the actual sunset. By actual sunrise we mean the actual crossing of the horizon by the Sun.

The apparent flattening of the Sun's disc at sunrise and sunset is also due to the same phenomenon.

Why do stars twinkle?

Why?

The atmosphere bends starlight towards the normal, so the apparent position of the star differs from its actual position. Since the physical conditions of the atmosphere are not stationary, the apparent position keeps fluctuating, the amount of starlight entering the eye flickers, and the star seems to twinkle.

Why do the planets not twinkle?

Why?

A planet is much closer to the earth than a star and is seen as an extended source. Treating it as a large collection of point-sized sources, the total variation in light from all of them averages out to zero, which nullifies the twinkling effect.

The path of a beam of light passing through a true solution is not visible. It becomes visible when it passes through a colloidal solution, where the size of the particles is relatively larger.

The earth's atmosphere is a heterogeneous mixture of minute particles: smoke, tiny water droplets, suspended particles of dust and molecules of air. When a beam of light strikes such fine particles, the path of the beam becomes visible, because the light reaches us after being reflected diffusely by these particles. This scattering of light by colloidal particles gives rise to the Tyndall effect.

The Tyndall effect is seen when a fine beam of sunlight enters a smoke-filled room through a small hole, and also when sunlight passes through the canopy of a dense forest, where tiny water droplets in the mist scatter the light.

The colour of the scattered light depends on the size of the scattering particles. Very fine particles scatter mainly blue light, while particles of larger size scatter light of longer wavelengths. If the particles are large enough, the scattered light may even appear white.

The molecules of air and other fine particles in the atmosphere are smaller than the wavelength of visible light. They are more effective in scattering light of shorter wavelengths at the blue end than light of longer wavelengths at the red end.

Red light has a wavelength about 1.8 times greater than blue light. So when sunlight passes through the atmosphere, the fine particles in the air scatter the blue colour more strongly than the red, and the scattered blue light enters our eyes.

If the earth had no atmosphere there would be no scattering at all, and the sky would look dark. That is why the sky appears dark to passengers flying at very high altitudes, where scattering is not prominent.

You may have noticed that danger signal lights are red. Red is the least scattered by fog or smoke, so a red light can be seen in the same colour from a distance.

Why does the sky appear dark instead of blue to an astronaut?

Why?

The blue colour of the sky comes from the scattering of sunlight by fine particles in the atmosphere. With no atmosphere there is no scattering, so the sky looks dark. For the same reason the sky appears dark to passengers flying at very high altitudes.

Our eyes can live even after our death. By donating our eyes after we die, we can light the life of a blind person.

About 35 million people in the developing world are blind, and most of them can be cured. About 4.5 million people with corneal blindness can be cured through corneal transplantation of donated eyes, and 60% of them are children below the age of 12.

Eye donors can belong to any age group or sex. People who use spectacles, or who have been operated on for cataract, can still donate their eyes.

Eyes must be removed within 4-6 hours after death, and the nearest eye bank should be informed immediately. Eye removal takes only 10-15 minutes and does not lead to any disfigurement. One pair of eyes gives vision to up to four corneal blind people.

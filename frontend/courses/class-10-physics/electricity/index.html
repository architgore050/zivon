Electricity

After this lesson you can:

  • After this lesson you can explain what electric current is, state its unit, and use I = Q/t to find the charge flowing in a circuit.
  • After this lesson you can define potential difference and resistance, state Ohm's law, and calculate with V = IR and R = ρl/A.
  • After this lesson you can find the equivalent resistance of resistors joined in series or in parallel and solve circuit problems with them.
  • After this lesson you can calculate the heat produced by a current using H = I²Rt and the power and energy used by a device, including the cost in kilowatt hours.

You have seen a torch work. The cells inside push charge through the bulb, and the bulb glows only while the switch is on. Water flowing in a river is called a water current; in the same way, when electric charge flows through a conductor such as a metallic wire, we say there is an electric current in the conductor.

A switch makes a conducting link between the cell and the bulb. If the circuit is broken anywhere, the current stops flowing and the bulb does not glow. A continuous and closed path of an electric current is called an electric circuit.

Electric current is the amount of charge flowing through a particular area in unit time; in other words it is the rate of flow of electric charges. In metallic wires, electrons are the charges that flow. Electrons were not known when electricity was first observed, so current was taken to be the flow of positive charges, and by convention the direction of electric current is opposite to the direction of flow of electrons.

If a net charge Q flows across any cross-section of a conductor in time t, then I = Q/t. The SI unit of charge is the coulomb (C), nearly the charge of 6 × 10^18 electrons, and an electron has a negative charge of 1.6 × 10^-19 C. The SI unit of current is the ampere (A), where 1 A = 1 C/1 s; small currents are written as 1 mA = 10^-3 A or 1 µA = 10^-6 A.

An instrument called the ammeter measures electric current, and it is always connected in series in the circuit through which the current is to be measured. In the torch circuit the current flows from the positive terminal of the cell to the negative terminal through the bulb and the ammeter.

Worked example

A current of 0.5 A is drawn by a filament of an electric bulb for 10 minutes. Find the amount of electric charge that flows through the circuit.

  1. Write down what is given Current I = 0.5 A. Time t = 10 minutes.
  2. Put the time in seconds t = 10 min = 10 × 60 s = 600 s, because the ampere is coulombs per second.
  3. Use the definition of current I = Q/t, so Q = I × t.
  4. Substitute Q = 0.5 A × 600 s = 300 C.

Answer The amount of charge that flows through the circuit is 300 C.

What does an electric circuit mean?

Why?

A switch makes a conducting link between the cell and the bulb, so the path must be closed; if the circuit is broken anywhere the current stops and the bulb does not glow. Note also that electrons are negative, so the conventional current is taken opposite to the direction of electron flow, not along it.

Calculate the number of electrons constituting one coulomb of charge.

Why?

One electron carries a charge of 1.6 × 10^-19 C. So the number of electrons in 1 C is 1 / (1.6 × 10^-19), which is about 6.25 × 10^18, that is nearly 6 × 10^18 electrons. This is exactly why one coulomb is said to be the charge contained in nearly 6 × 10^18 electrons.

Charges do not flow in a copper wire by themselves, just as water in a perfectly horizontal tube does not flow. If one end of the tube is joined to a tank kept at a higher level, so that there is a pressure difference between the two ends, water flows out of the other end.

For charges in a metallic wire, gravity has no role to play. The electrons move only if there is a difference of electric pressure, called the potential difference, along the conductor. A battery, made of one or more cells, produces this difference.

The chemical action within a cell generates the potential difference across its terminals even when no current is drawn from it. When the cell is connected to a conducting circuit element, the potential difference sets the charges in motion and produces an electric current, and to keep the current flowing the cell has to expend its stored chemical energy.

The potential difference between two points in a circuit carrying some current is the work done to move a unit charge from one point to the other, so V = W/Q. Its SI unit is the volt (V): one volt is the potential difference between two points when 1 joule of work is done to move a charge of 1 coulomb, that is 1 V = 1 J C^-1.

The voltmeter measures potential difference, and it is always connected in parallel across the points between which the potential difference is to be measured.

Worked example

How much work is done in moving a charge of 2 C across two points having a potential difference 12 V?

  1. Write down what is given Charge Q = 2 C, potential difference V = 12 V.
  2. Use the definition of potential difference V = W/Q, so the work done is W = V × Q.
  3. Substitute W = 12 V × 2 C = 24 J.

Answer The work done is 24 J.

How much energy is given to each coulomb of charge passing through a 6 V battery?

Why?

One volt means one joule of work per coulomb, so a 6 V battery gives 6 J of energy to every coulomb that passes through it. The battery is the device that maintains this potential difference across the conductor.

A circuit contains a cell or a battery, a plug key, the electrical components and connecting wires. Instead of drawing the real objects, we draw a schematic diagram in which each component is replaced by a conventional symbol.

The symbols you will meet are: an electric cell; a battery, which is a combination of cells; a plug key or switch, shown open or closed; a wire joint; wires crossing without joining; an electric bulb; a resistor of resistance R; a variable resistance or rheostat; an ammeter; and a voltmeter.

Two of these symbols carry a rule with them. The ammeter goes in series with the part whose current you are measuring, and the voltmeter goes in parallel with the part whose potential difference you are measuring.

How is a voltmeter connected in the circuit to measure the potential difference between two points?

Why?

A voltmeter is always connected in parallel across the points between which the potential difference is to be measured. The ammeter is the one that goes in series, because it must carry the current it is measuring.

Set up a circuit with a nichrome wire XY of length about 0.5 m, an ammeter, a voltmeter and four cells of 1.5 V each. Start with one cell and note the ammeter reading I and the voltmeter reading V across XY; then repeat with two, three and four cells. For each pair, work out V/I, and plot V against I.

In the activity you find approximately the same value of V/I in every case, and the V-I graph is a straight line passing through the origin. So V/I is a constant ratio for that wire.

In 1827 the German physicist Georg Simon Ohm found the relationship between the current in a metallic wire and the potential difference across its ends. The potential difference V across the ends of a given metallic wire is directly proportional to the current I through it, provided its temperature remains the same. This is Ohm's law: V is proportional to I, so V/I = constant = R, and V = IR.

R is a constant for the given metallic wire at a given temperature and is called its resistance, the property of a conductor to resist the flow of charges through it. Its SI unit is the ohm (Ω). If the potential difference across a conductor is 1 V and the current through it is 1 A, its resistance is 1 Ω, that is 1 ohm = 1 volt/1 ampere.

Because V = IR can be written I = V/R, the current through a resistor is inversely proportional to its resistance: if the resistance is doubled the current gets halved. In many practical cases we need to increase or decrease the current, and a component used to regulate current without changing the voltage source is called a variable resistance; a device called a rheostat is often used in a circuit to change the resistance.

V-I graph of a nichrome wire

A nichrome wire is connected to a number of 1.5 V cells, with an ammeter and a voltmeter. Add cells and change the wire resistance, and watch where each (current, potential difference) point falls on the graph.

Worked example

(a) How much current will an electric bulb draw from a 220 V source, if the resistance of the bulb filament is 1200 Ω? (b) How much current will an electric heater coil draw from a 220 V source, if the resistance of the heater coil is 100 Ω?

  1. Write down what is given for the bulb V = 220 V, R = 1200 Ω.
  2. Use Ohm's law in the form I = V/R I = 220 V / 1200 Ω = 0.18 A.
  3. Write down what is given for the heater V = 220 V, R = 100 Ω.
  4. Use I = V/R again I = 220 V / 100 Ω = 2.2 A.

Answer The bulb draws about 0.18 A and the heater coil draws 2.2 A from the same 220 V source. Note the difference of current drawn by a bulb and a heater from the same source: the smaller the resistance, the larger the current.

Worked example

The potential difference between the terminals of an electric heater is 60 V when it draws a current of 4 A from the source. What current will the heater draw if the potential difference is increased to 120 V?

  1. Find the resistance first The resistance of the heater stays the same. From Ohm's law, R = V/I = 60 V / 4 A = 15 Ω.
  2. Now change the potential difference With V = 120 V and R = 15 Ω, the current is I = V/R.
  3. Substitute I = 120 V / 15 Ω = 8 A.

Answer The current through the heater becomes 8 A when the potential difference is doubled.

When a 12 V battery is connected across an unknown resistor, there is a current of 2.5 mA in the circuit. Find the value of the resistance of the resistor, in ohms.

Why?

First change the current to amperes: 2.5 mA = 2.5 × 10^-3 A. Then R = V/I = 12 V / (2.5 × 10^-3 A) = 4800 Ω.

Take a circuit with a cell, an ammeter, a nichrome wire of length l and a plug key, and note the current. Now replace the wire with another nichrome wire of the same thickness but twice the length, and note the current again: the ammeter reading decreases to one-half.

Repeat with a thicker nichrome wire of the same length, so that its cross-sectional area is larger: the ammeter reading increases. Now use a copper wire of the same length and the same area of cross-section as the first nichrome wire, and the reading changes again.

These observations show that the resistance of a conductor depends on its length, on its area of cross-section, and on the nature of its material. Precise measurements show that the resistance of a uniform metallic conductor is directly proportional to its length l and inversely proportional to its area of cross-section A, so R = ρl/A.

The constant ρ (rho) is called the electrical resistivity of the material of the conductor. Its SI unit is Ω m, and it is a characteristic property of the material. Metals and alloys have very low resistivity, in the range 10^-8 Ω m to 10^-6 Ω m, and are good conductors of electricity; insulators like rubber and glass have resistivity of the order of 10^12 to 10^17 Ω m. Both the resistance and the resistivity of a material vary with temperature.

The resistivity of an alloy is generally higher than that of its constituent metals, and alloys do not oxidise (burn) readily at high temperatures; for this reason they are commonly used in electrical heating devices like electric irons and toasters. Tungsten is used almost exclusively for filaments of electric bulbs, while copper and aluminium are generally used for electrical transmission lines.

Worked example

Resistance of a metal wire of length 1 m is 26 Ω at 20 degrees C. If the diameter of the wire is 0.3 mm, what will be the resistivity of the metal at that temperature? Using the table of resistivities, predict the material of the wire.

  1. Write down what is given R = 26 Ω, length l = 1 m, diameter d = 0.3 mm = 3 × 10^-4 m.
  2. Find the area of cross-section The wire is round, so A = πd²/4.
  3. Rearrange R = ρl/A The resistivity is ρ = RA/l = Rπd²/(4l).
  4. Substitute Putting in the values gives ρ = 1.84 × 10^-6 Ω m.
  5. Name the material Looking at the table of resistivities at 20 degrees C, the value 1.84 × 10^-6 Ω m is the resistivity of manganese.

Answer The resistivity of the metal at 20 degrees C is 1.84 × 10^-6 Ω m, which is the resistivity of manganese.

Worked example

A wire of given material having length l and area of cross-section A has a resistance of 4 Ω. What would be the resistance of another wire of the same material having length l/2 and area of cross-section 2A?

  1. Write the first wire For the first wire, R1 = ρl/A = 4 Ω.
  2. Write the second wire For the second wire, R2 = ρ(l/2)/(2A) = (1/4) ρl/A.
  3. Compare the two So R2 = (1/4) R1.
  4. Substitute R2 = (1/4) × 4 Ω = 1 Ω.

Answer The resistance of the new wire is 1 Ω.

Will current flow more easily through a thick wire or a thin wire of the same material, when connected to the same source? Why?

Why?

Resistance is inversely proportional to the area of cross-section, R = ρl/A. A thicker wire of the same material and length has the larger area, so its resistance is smaller and the current is larger. Resistivity depends on the material only, not on the thickness.

When three resistors R1, R2 and R3 are joined end to end, they are said to be connected in series. If you move the ammeter to any position in such a circuit, its reading does not change: in a series combination the current is the same in every part of the circuit, so the same current passes through each resistor.

Measure the potential difference across the whole combination and call it V, then measure the potential differences V1, V2 and V3 across the three resistors separately. You find that V = V1 + V2 + V3: the total potential difference across a series combination is the sum of the potential differences across the individual resistors.

Now replace the three resistors by one equivalent resistor of resistance R that carries the same current I with the same potential difference V across it, so that V = IR. Applying Ohm's law to the three resistors separately gives V1 = IR1, V2 = IR2 and V3 = IR3.

Putting these into V = V1 + V2 + V3 gives IR = IR1 + IR2 + IR3, and dividing by I gives Rs = R1 + R2 + R3. So when several resistors are joined in series, the resistance of the combination equals the sum of their individual resistances, and it is greater than any individual resistance.

Why Rs = R1 + R2 + R3

Start from the observed sum

The experiment shows the total potential difference is V = V1 + V2 + V3 across the series combination.

Replace the combination by one resistor

Let one equivalent resistor R carry the same current I with the same total potential difference V, so Ohm's law for the whole circuit gives V = IR.

Apply Ohm's law to each resistor

The current through each resistor is the same I, so V1 = IR1, V2 = IR2 and V3 = IR3.

Substitute and cancel I

IR = IR1 + IR2 + IR3, and dividing both sides by I gives Rs = R1 + R2 + R3.

Read the result

The equivalent resistance is the plain sum, so it is larger than any single resistance in the combination.

Worked example

An electric lamp, whose resistance is 20 Ω, and a conductor of 4 Ω resistance are connected to a 6 V battery. Calculate (a) the total resistance of the circuit, (b) the current through the circuit, and (c) the potential difference across the electric lamp and conductor.

  1. Add the series resistances The lamp R1 = 20 Ω and the conductor R2 = 4 Ω are in series, so Rs = 20 Ω + 4 Ω = 24 Ω.
  2. Find the current The battery gives V = 6 V, so I = V/Rs = 6 V / 24 Ω = 0.25 A. This same current flows through both.
  3. Potential difference across the lamp V1 = R1 × I = 20 Ω × 0.25 A = 5 V.
  4. Potential difference across the conductor V2 = R2 × I = 4 Ω × 0.25 A = 1 V. Check: 5 V + 1 V = 6 V, the battery voltage.
  5. Check the equivalent resistance A single resistor replacing the two must give 0.25 A at 6 V, so R = V/I = 6 V / 0.25 A = 24 Ω, the same as the total series resistance.

Answer Total resistance 24 Ω, current 0.25 A, potential difference 5 V across the lamp and 1 V across the conductor.

A battery of three cells of 2 V each is connected in series with a 5 Ω resistor, an 8 Ω resistor, a 12 Ω resistor and a plug key. What current flows through the resistors? Give the answer in amperes.

Why?

Three cells of 2 V each give 6 V. The series resistance is 5 + 8 + 12 = 25 Ω. So I = V/Rs = 6 V / 25 Ω = 0.24 A, and this same current flows through every resistor. A voltmeter connected across the 12 Ω resistor would read 12 Ω × 0.24 A = 2.88 V.

When three resistors are connected together between the same two points X and Y, they are said to be connected in parallel. Now the potential difference across each resistor is the same, but the current divides.

Measure the total current I entering the combination, and then the separate currents I1, I2 and I3 through the three branches. You find I = I1 + I2 + I3: the total current is the sum of the separate currents through each branch of the combination.

Let Rp be the equivalent resistance of the parallel combination. Applying Ohm's law to the whole combination gives I = V/Rp, and applying it to each resistor gives I1 = V/R1, I2 = V/R2 and I3 = V/R3.

Substituting these into I = I1 + I2 + I3 gives V/Rp = V/R1 + V/R2 + V/R3, and dividing by V gives 1/Rp = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3. The reciprocal of the equivalent resistance of a group of resistors joined in parallel equals the sum of the reciprocals of the individual resistances, so the total resistance in a parallel circuit is decreased.

The two arrangements behave very differently. In a series circuit the current is constant throughout, so it is impracticable to connect an electric bulb and an electric heater in series, because they need currents of widely different values to operate properly. Another disadvantage of a series circuit is that when one component fails the circuit is broken and none of the components works, as you see when a single dead bulb in a string of fairy lights puts out the whole string. A parallel circuit, on the other hand, divides the current through the gadgets, which helps when each gadget has a different resistance and needs a different current.

Why 1/Rp = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3

Start from the observed sum

The experiment shows the total current is I = I1 + I2 + I3 into the parallel combination.

Replace the combination by one resistor

Let one equivalent resistor Rp carry the same total current I at the same potential difference V, so I = V/Rp.

Apply Ohm's law to each branch

Every branch has the same potential difference V, so I1 = V/R1, I2 = V/R2 and I3 = V/R3.

Substitute and cancel V

V/Rp = V/R1 + V/R2 + V/R3, and dividing both sides by V gives 1/Rp = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3.

Read the result

We add the reciprocals, so the equivalent resistance comes out smaller than the smallest individual resistance.

Worked example

Three resistors R1, R2 and R3 have the values 5 Ω, 10 Ω and 30 Ω respectively, and are connected in parallel to a battery of 12 V. Calculate (a) the current through each resistor, (b) the total current in the circuit, and (c) the total circuit resistance.

  1. Note the potential difference The battery gives V = 12 V, and this is also the potential difference across each individual resistor because they are in parallel.
  2. Current through R1 I1 = V/R1 = 12 V / 5 Ω = 2.4 A.
  3. Current through R2 I2 = V/R2 = 12 V / 10 Ω = 1.2 A.
  4. Current through R3 I3 = V/R3 = 12 V / 30 Ω = 0.4 A.
  5. Total current I = I1 + I2 + I3 = 2.4 + 1.2 + 0.4 = 4 A.
  6. Total resistance 1/Rp = 1/5 + 1/10 + 1/30 = 1/3, so Rp = 3 Ω. Check: V/I = 12 V / 4 A = 3 Ω.

Answer The branch currents are 2.4 A, 1.2 A and 0.4 A; the total current is 4 A; the total resistance of the parallel combination is 3 Ω.

Worked example

In a circuit with a 12 V battery, R1 = 10 Ω and R2 = 40 Ω are in parallel with each other, and R3 = 30 Ω, R4 = 20 Ω and R5 = 60 Ω are in parallel with each other; the two parallel groups are joined in series. Calculate (a) the total resistance in the circuit and (b) the total current flowing in the circuit.

  1. Replace the first parallel pair 1/R' = 1/10 + 1/40 = 5/40, so R' = 8 Ω.
  2. Replace the second parallel group 1/R'' = 1/30 + 1/20 + 1/60 = 6/60, so R'' = 10 Ω.
  3. Add the two groups in series The total resistance is R = R' + R'' = 8 Ω + 10 Ω = 18 Ω.
  4. Find the total current By Ohm's law, I = V/R = 12 V / 18 Ω = 0.67 A.

Answer The total resistance is 18 Ω and the total current is about 0.67 A.

What are the advantages of connecting electrical devices in parallel with the battery instead of connecting them in series?

Why?

In a parallel circuit every gadget gets the same potential difference and its own current, which suits gadgets of different resistances, and the total resistance is decreased. In a series circuit a single failure breaks the circuit and nothing works, which is why series wiring is not used for domestic circuits.

A cell or battery is a source of electrical energy, and to maintain a current the source keeps expending energy. Part of that energy may be used in useful work, like rotating the blades of an electric fan, and the rest may be expended as heat, which is why a fan becomes warm when used continuously for a long time.

If the electric circuit is purely resistive, that is, a configuration of resistors only connected to a battery, the source energy continually gets dissipated entirely in the form of heat. This is known as the heating effect of electric current, and it is used in devices such as electric heaters and electric irons.

Consider a current I flowing through a resistor of resistance R with a potential difference V across it, and let a charge Q flow in time t. The work done in moving the charge Q through the potential difference V is VQ, so the source must supply energy VQ in time t, and the power input to the circuit is P = VQ/t = VI. The energy supplied by the source in time t is therefore VIt, and this energy is dissipated in the resistor as heat, so H = VIt.

Applying Ohm's law V = IR to this gives H = I²Rt. This is Joule's law of heating, and it says that the heat produced in a resistor is directly proportional to the square of the current for a given resistance, directly proportional to the resistance for a given current, and directly proportional to the time for which the current flows.

From H = VIt to Joule's law H = I²Rt

Energy supplied by the source

In time t a charge Q flows, and the work done in moving it through the potential difference V is VQ, so the source supplies energy VQ in time t.

Write the power input

Since I = Q/t, the power input is P = VQ/t = VI, so the energy supplied in time t is VIt.

This energy becomes heat

In a purely resistive circuit the whole of it is dissipated in the resistor as heat, so H = VIt.

Use Ohm's law to remove V

Put V = IR into H = VIt, giving H = (IR)It = I²Rt.

Read the three proportionalities

For a fixed resistance, heat goes as the square of the current; for a fixed current, heat goes as the resistance; and for both fixed, heat goes as the time.

Worked example

An electric iron consumes energy at a rate of 840 W when heating is at the maximum rate and 360 W when the heating is at the minimum. The voltage is 220 V. What are the current and the resistance in each case?

  1. Start from the power relation The power input is P = VI, so the current is I = P/V, and the resistance follows from R = V/I.
  2. Maximum heating rate: current I = 840 W / 220 V = 3.82 A.
  3. Maximum heating rate: resistance R = V/I = 220 V / 3.82 A = 57.60 Ω.
  4. Minimum heating rate: current I = 360 W / 220 V = 1.64 A.
  5. Minimum heating rate: resistance R = V/I = 220 V / 1.64 A = 134.15 Ω.

Answer At the maximum rate the current is 3.82 A and the resistance is 57.60 Ω; at the minimum rate the current is 1.64 A and the resistance is 134.15 Ω.

Worked example

100 J of heat is produced each second in a 4 Ω resistance. Find the potential difference across the resistor.

  1. Write down what is given Heat H = 100 J, resistance R = 4 Ω, time t = 1 s. We want V.
  2. Use Joule's law to get the current H = I²Rt, so I = square root of (H / (Rt)) = square root of (100 J / (4 Ω × 1 s)) = 5 A.
  3. Find the potential difference From Ohm's law V = IR = 5 A × 4 Ω = 20 V.

Answer The potential difference across the resistor is 20 V.

An electric iron of resistance 20 Ω takes a current of 5 A. Calculate the heat developed in 30 s. Give the answer in joules.

Why?

Use Joule's law H = I²Rt = (5 A)² × 20 Ω × 30 s = 25 × 20 × 30 = 15000 J.

The generation of heat in a conductor is an inevitable consequence of electric current, and in many cases it is undesirable because useful electrical energy turns into heat and the components heat up. But the heating effect has many useful applications: the electric laundry iron, electric toaster, electric oven, electric kettle and electric heater are all based on Joule's heating.

The electric bulb uses heating to produce light. The filament must retain as much of the generated heat as possible so that it becomes very hot and emits light, and it must not melt at that temperature. A strong metal with a high melting point, such as tungsten (melting point 3380 °C), is used for bulb filaments, the filament is thermally isolated as much as possible using insulating supports, and the bulb is filled with chemically inactive nitrogen and argon gases to prolong the life of the filament. Most of the power consumed by the filament appears as heat, and only a small part is radiated as light.

The fuse is another common application of Joule's heating. It protects circuits and appliances by stopping the flow of any unduly high current, and it is placed in series with the device. It consists of a piece of wire made of a metal or an alloy of appropriate melting point, for example aluminium, copper, iron or lead, usually encased in a cartridge of porcelain or similar material with metal ends. If a current larger than the specified value flows, the temperature of the fuse wire rises, the wire melts and the circuit is broken.

Fuses for domestic purposes are rated 1 A, 2 A, 3 A, 5 A, 10 A, and so on, and the rating has to be chosen from the current the appliance draws. For an electric iron which consumes 1 kW electric power when operated at 220 V, a current of (1000/220) A, that is 4.54 A, will flow in the circuit. In this case a 5 A fuse must be used: the fuse wire melts only when a current larger than its specified value flows, so the rating must lie above the working current of 4.54 A, and 3 A would be too low.

An electric iron consumes 1 kW of electric power when operated at 220 V. Which fuse rating must be used with it?

Why?

A current of (1000/220) A, that is 4.54 A, flows in the circuit. The fuse wire melts only when a current larger than its specified value flows, so a 3 A fuse would be below the working current and a 5 A fuse must be used.

The rate of doing work is power, and this is also the rate of consumption of energy. The rate at which electric energy is dissipated or consumed in an electric circuit is called electric power, and it is given by P = VI. Using Ohm's law this can also be written P = I²R or P = V²/R.

The SI unit of electric power is the watt (W). It is the power consumed by a device that carries 1 A of current when operated at a potential difference of 1 V, so 1 W = 1 volt × 1 ampere = 1 V A.

The watt is a very small unit, so in actual practice we use a much larger unit called the kilowatt, which is equal to 1000 watts. Electrical energy is the product of power and time, so its unit is the watt hour (W h), the energy consumed when 1 watt of power is used for 1 hour. The commercial unit of electric energy is the kilowatt hour (kW h), commonly known as a unit: 1 kW h = 1000 watt × 3600 second = 3.6 × 10^6 watt second = 3.6 × 10^6 joule.

One caution: many people think that electrons are consumed in an electric circuit. This is wrong. We pay the electricity board or electric company to provide energy to move electrons through gadgets like the electric bulb, fan and engines; we pay for the energy that we use.

Worked example

An electric bulb is connected to a 220 V generator. The current is 0.50 A. What is the power of the bulb?

  1. Write down what is given V = 220 V, I = 0.50 A.
  2. Use the power relation P = VI.
  3. Substitute P = 220 V × 0.50 A = 110 J/s = 110 W.

Answer The power of the bulb is 110 W.

Worked example

An electric refrigerator rated 400 W operates 8 hour/day. What is the cost of the energy to operate it for 30 days at Rs 3.00 per kW h?

  1. Find the total energy in watt hours 400 W × 8.0 hour/day × 30 days = 96000 W h.
  2. Convert to kilowatt hours 96000 W h = 96 kW h, since 1 kW h = 1000 W h.
  3. Multiply by the rate 96 kW h × Rs 3.00 per kW h = Rs 288.00.

Answer The cost of running the refrigerator for 30 days is Rs 288.00.

Which of the following terms does not represent electrical power in a circuit?

Why?

Electric power is P = VI, which becomes P = I²R when V = IR is put in, and P = V²/R when I = V/R is put in. The combination IR² has the wrong powers of I and R, so it is not a power.

A piece of wire of resistance R is cut into five equal parts. These parts are then connected in parallel. If the equivalent resistance of this combination is R', then what is the ratio R/R'?

Why?

Each of the five equal parts has resistance R/5. Five such resistors in parallel have 1/R' = 5/(R/5) = 25/R, so R' = R/25. Therefore R/R' = 25.

An electric bulb is rated 220 V and 100 W. When it is operated on 110 V, what power will it consume?

Why?

The resistance of the bulb is fixed: R = V²/P = (220 V)² / 100 W = 484 Ω. At 110 V the power is P = V²/R = (110 V)² / 484 Ω = 25 W. Halving the voltage quarters the power, because power goes as V² for a fixed resistance.

Two conducting wires of the same material and of equal lengths and equal diameters are first connected in series and then in parallel in a circuit across the same potential difference. What is the ratio of the heat produced in the series combination to that in the parallel combination?

Why?

Each wire has resistance R. In series the resistance is 2R; in parallel it is R/2. For the same potential difference V and the same time t, the heat is V²t/R, so it is inversely proportional to the resistance. The ratio of heats is (1/2R) : (1/(R/2)) = 1 : 4.

How many 176 Ω resistors (in parallel) are required to carry 5 A on a 220 V line?

Why?

The combination must have resistance R = V/I = 220 V / 5 A = 44 Ω. If n resistors of 176 Ω are joined in parallel, R = 176/n, so n = 176/44 = 4.

Glossary

Coulomb
The SI unit of electric charge; one coulomb is nearly the charge of 6 × 10^18 electrons.
Ampere
The SI unit of electric current; one ampere is one coulomb of charge flowing per second.
Volt
The SI unit of potential difference; one volt is one joule of work per coulomb of charge moved.
Ohm
The SI unit of resistance; a conductor has resistance 1 ohm when a potential difference of 1 volt drives a current of 1 ampere through it.
Resistivity
A property of the material of a conductor, with SI unit Ω m, that tells how strongly that material resists current for a given length and area.
Equivalent resistance
The single resistance that can replace a combination of resistors while keeping the same current and potential difference for the whole circuit.
Joule's law of heating
The rule H = I²Rt giving the heat produced in a resistor by a steady current.
Watt
The SI unit of power; one watt is consumed when a current of 1 ampere flows at a potential difference of 1 volt.
Kilowatt hour
The commercial unit of electrical energy, equal to 3.6 × 10^6 J.
Fuse
A short wire of suitable melting point placed in series with a device, which melts and breaks the circuit when the current becomes too large.

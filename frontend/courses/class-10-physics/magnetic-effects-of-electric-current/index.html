Magnetic Effects of Electric Current

Hold a small compass near a straight copper wire. Before current flows, the needle rests in one direction. When you pass current through the wire, the needle deflects. This simple observation is the door into this chapter.

The deflection means that the electric current through the copper wire has produced a magnetic effect. Electricity and magnetism are linked. A current-carrying wire behaves like a magnet.

In 1820 Hans Christian Oersted accidentally found that a compass needle got deflected when an electric current passed through a metallic wire placed nearby. This showed that electricity and magnetism were related phenomena.

Oersted's name is used for the unit of magnetic field strength, the oersted.

A compass needle placed near a straight copper wire deflects when current is passed through the wire. What does this show?

Why?

The deflection of the compass needle shows that the current through the copper wire has produced a magnetic effect.

A compass needle is a small bar magnet. Its north-seeking end points approximately north and is called the north pole; its south-seeking end points approximately south and is called the south pole. Like poles repel, while unlike poles attract.

Bring a magnet near iron filings and the filings arrange in a pattern. The magnet exerts a force in the region around it. That region is said to have a magnetic field.

The lines along which the iron filings align are magnetic field lines. The direction of the magnetic field at a point is the direction in which a north pole of a compass needle moves there.

Field lines emerge from the north pole and merge at the south pole. Inside the magnet, they run from south pole to north pole, so field lines are closed curves.

The closeness of field lines shows strength: crowded lines mean a stronger field. No two field lines cross, because at a crossing a compass needle would have to point in two directions at once, which is impossible.

Why does a compass needle get deflected when brought near a bar magnet?

Why?

A compass needle is a small bar magnet, so a nearby magnet's field exerts a force on it and turns it.

How to draw magnetic field lines around a bar magnet

Fix the paper and magnet

Fix a sheet of white paper on a drawing board and place a bar magnet in the centre. Mark the boundary of the magnet.

Start near the north pole

Place a small compass near the north pole of the magnet. The south pole of the needle points towards the north pole of the magnet, and the north pole of the compass is directed away from the magnet's north pole.

Mark and step forward

Mark the position of the two ends of the needle. Move the compass to a new position so that its south pole occupies the position previously occupied by its north pole.

Continue to the south pole

Proceed step by step until you reach the south pole of the magnet. Join the points marked on the paper by a smooth curve. This curve represents a field line.

Draw more lines

Repeat the procedure and draw as many lines as you can. You get a pattern of magnetic field lines around the magnet.

Which of the following is a property of magnetic field lines?

Why?

Field lines emerge from the north pole and merge at the south pole; inside the magnet they run from south to north, so they form closed curves. No two field lines cross.

Why don't two magnetic field lines intersect each other?

Why?

At a crossing, the field would have two directions at the same point, so a compass needle would have to point in two directions at once.

Which two methods can produce a magnetic field?

Why?

A magnet has a magnetic field around it, and a current-carrying conductor also produces a magnetic field.

Pass current through a long straight copper wire. Sprinkle iron filings on a cardboard through which the wire passes. The filings form concentric circles around the wire. These circles are the magnetic field lines.

If you reverse the current, the direction of the field lines reverses. If you increase the current, the compass deflection increases, so the field is stronger. If you move the compass farther from the wire, the deflection decreases, so the field is weaker.

The field lines around a straight current-carrying wire are concentric circles centred on the wire.

Which of the following correctly describes the magnetic field near a long straight wire?

Why?

Iron filings around a straight current-carrying wire arrange themselves in concentric circles centred on the wire.

Using the right-hand thumb rule

Hold the conductor

Imagine holding the current-carrying straight conductor in your right hand.

Point the thumb

Point your thumb towards the direction of the current.

Curl the fingers

Your fingers wrap around the conductor in the direction of the field lines of the magnetic field.

Remember the other name

This rule is also called Maxwell's corkscrew rule.

Worked example

A current through a horizontal power line flows in east to west direction. What is the direction of magnetic field at a point directly below it and at a point directly above it?

  1. Identify the current direction The current is in the east-west direction.
  2. Apply the right-hand thumb rule Point the thumb of the right hand along the current; the fingers curl around the wire in the direction of the magnetic field.
  3. View from the east end Viewed from the east end, the magnetic field at any point below or above the wire turns clockwise in a plane perpendicular to the wire.
  4. View from the west end Viewed from the west end, the same field turns anti-clockwise.

Answer The magnetic field at any point below or above the wire turns clockwise when viewed from the east end, and anti-clockwise when viewed from the west end.

If the current in a straight wire is reversed, what happens to the magnetic field lines around it?

Why?

Reversing the current reverses the direction of the magnetic field produced by the current.

Bend the straight wire into a circular loop. At every point of the loop, the field lines are circles around the wire. As you move away from the wire, these circles become larger. At the centre of the loop, the arcs of these large circles appear as straight lines. Every section of the wire adds its field in the same direction inside the loop.

If the coil has n turns, the field is n times as large as that of a single turn, because each turn carries current in the same direction and the fields add up.

A circular loop of wire lies in the plane of a table. The current in the loop is clockwise when viewed from above. Using the right-hand rule, what is the direction of the magnetic field inside the loop?

Why?

Curl the fingers of the right hand in the clockwise direction of the current; the thumb points into the table. Outside the loop the field lines return in the opposite direction.

A solenoid is a coil with many circular turns wound in the shape of a cylinder. Its field lines are similar to those of a bar magnet. Inside the solenoid the field lines are straight and the field is uniform. One end acts as a north pole and the other as a south pole.

An electromagnet is made of a soft iron core wrapped with a coil of insulated copper wire. A current-carrying solenoid can magnetise a steel rod placed inside it.

The magnetic field inside a long straight solenoid-carrying current

Why?

Inside a long current-carrying solenoid the field lines are straight and the field is uniform, so it is the same at all points inside.

The magnetic field in a given region is uniform. How is it represented by field lines?

Why?

A uniform field has the same strength and direction everywhere, so its field lines are parallel and equally spaced straight lines.

A current-carrying conductor placed in a magnetic field experiences a force. Suspend a small aluminium rod horizontally between the poles of a strong horse-shoe magnet and pass current through it. The rod is displaced. Reverse the current and the displacement reverses. Reverse the magnetic field and the force reverses again.

The force is largest when the current is at right angles to the magnetic field. Devices that use current-carrying conductors and magnetic fields include electric motors, electric generators, loudspeakers, microphones and measuring instruments.

Using Fleming's left-hand rule

Stretch the fingers

Stretch the thumb, forefinger and middle finger of your left hand so that they are mutually perpendicular.

Point the first finger

Point the first finger in the direction of the magnetic field.

Point the second finger

Point the second finger in the direction of the current.

Read the thumb

The thumb then points in the direction of motion or the force acting on the conductor.

Worked example

In Activity 12.7, a small aluminium rod AB is suspended horizontally from a stand. A strong horse-shoe magnet is placed so that its north pole is vertically below and its south pole is vertically above the rod. A current is passed through the rod from end B to end A, and the rod is displaced towards the left. Explain why the rod is displaced, and state what is observed if the direction of the current through the rod is reversed.

  1. Note the direction of the magnetic field The north pole of the magnet is vertically below the rod and the south pole is vertically above it, so the magnetic field between the poles is directed upwards.
  2. Note the direction of the current The current is passed through the rod from end B to end A.
  3. Apply the force rule A current-carrying conductor placed in a magnetic field experiences a force. The force is perpendicular to both the direction of the current and the direction of the magnetic field, so we use Fleming's left-hand rule to find its direction.
  4. Read the direction with Fleming's left-hand rule Point the first finger along the magnetic field (upwards) and the second finger along the current (from B to A). The thumb then gives the direction of the force on the rod, which is why the rod is displaced towards the left.
  5. Reverse the current The direction of the force is reversed when the direction of current through the conductor is reversed. So the displacement is now towards the right. (The force is largest when the current is at right angles to the magnetic field, as it is here.)

Answer The rod is displaced towards the left because the current-carrying rod experiences a force in the magnetic field, with direction given by Fleming's left-hand rule. Reversing the current reverses the direction of the force, so the rod is then displaced towards the right.

In homes, electric power comes through mains, either by overhead poles or underground cables. The live wire usually has red insulation and is positive. The neutral wire has black insulation and is negative. In our country the potential difference between them is 220 V. The supply is AC at 220 V with a frequency of 50 Hz.

At the meter-board, the wires pass through an electricity meter and a main fuse. Through the main switch they connect to line wires. Often two circuits are used: a 15 A circuit for high-power appliances such as geysers and air coolers, and a 5 A circuit for bulbs and fans.

The earth wire has green insulation and is connected to a metal plate deep in the earth. It is a safety measure for appliances with metallic bodies. It provides a low-resistance path so that any leakage of current to the metallic body does not give the user a severe shock.

Appliances are connected in parallel across live and neutral so that each gets the same potential difference. Each appliance has its own switch. A fuse prevents damage due to overloading. The Joule heating in the fuse melts it and breaks the circuit.

Short-circuiting occurs when the live and neutral wires touch directly; the current increases abruptly. Overloading can also occur from an accidental hike in supply voltage or by connecting too many appliances to one socket.

Electric currents in the body, even weak ion currents along nerve cells, produce magnetic fields. These fields are very weak, about one-billionth of the earth's magnetic field. The heart and the brain are two main organs where the magnetic field produced is significant.

Glossary

magnetic field
The region around a magnet where its force can be detected.
magnetic field line
A line that shows the path a hypothetical free north pole would tend to move along; it represents a magnetic field.
right-hand thumb rule
A rule for the direction of the field around a straight current-carrying wire: thumb along the current, curled fingers along the field lines.
Fleming's left-hand rule
A rule for the force on a current-carrying conductor: forefinger along the field, middle finger along the current, thumb along the force.
solenoid
A cylindrical coil with many turns of wire; its field is like that of a bar magnet.
electromagnet
A soft iron core wrapped with a coil of insulated copper wire, which becomes magnetic when current flows.
live wire
The red-insulated domestic supply wire, called positive.
neutral wire
The black-insulated domestic supply wire, called negative.
earth wire
The green-insulated safety wire connected to a metal plate deep in the earth.
fuse
A safety device that melts and breaks the circuit when current becomes too high.
short circuit
A direct contact between live and neutral wires that makes current increase abruptly.
MRI
Magnetic Resonance Imaging, a technique that uses the body's magnetic field to make images for diagnosis.

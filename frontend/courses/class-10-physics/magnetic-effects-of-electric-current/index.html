Magnetic Effects of Electric Current

After this lesson you can:

  • After this lesson you can describe the magnetic field of a magnet and list the properties of magnetic field lines.
  • After this lesson you can find the direction of the magnetic field produced by a current in a straight wire, a circular loop and a solenoid using the right-hand thumb rule.
  • After this lesson you can use Fleming's left-hand rule to find the direction of the force on a current-carrying conductor placed in a magnetic field.
  • After this lesson you can explain the live, neutral and earth wires of a domestic circuit, and why a fuse protects the circuit from short-circuiting and overloading.

You already know one effect of an electric current: it heats the wire it flows through. There is a second effect. A wire carrying a current also behaves like a magnet, and you can see this with a compass.

Take a thick straight copper wire and connect it between two points, X and Y, of an electric circuit. Keep the wire perpendicular to the plane of the paper. Place a small compass horizontally near the wire and note the position of its needle.

Now insert the key so that current flows, and look at the needle again. The needle is deflected. The electric current through the copper wire has produced a magnetic effect. Electricity and magnetism are linked to each other.

This link was found by accident. In 1820, Hans Christian Oersted discovered that a compass needle got deflected when an electric current passed through a metallic wire placed nearby. His observation showed that electricity and magnetism were related phenomena.

Why does a compass needle get deflected when it is brought near a bar magnet?

Why?

A compass needle is a small bar magnet. A bar magnet produces a magnetic field around itself, and inside that field a force acts on another magnet, so the needle turns.

A compass needle is a small bar magnet. Its two ends point approximately towards the north and south directions. The end that points towards the north is called the north-seeking or north pole; the other end, which points towards the south, is called the south-seeking or south pole. Like poles repel each other, and unlike poles attract each other.

Fix a sheet of white paper on a drawing board. Place a bar magnet in the centre and sprinkle iron filings uniformly around it. Tap the board gently. The iron filings arrange themselves in a pattern.

Why do they arrange in a pattern? The magnet exerts its influence in the region around it, and the filings experience a force. The region surrounding a magnet in which the force of the magnet can be detected is said to have a magnetic field. The lines along which the iron filings align themselves represent magnetic field lines.

Four properties follow. First, the direction of the magnetic field at a point is the direction in which a north pole of the compass needle would move there. By convention, field lines emerge from the north pole and merge at the south pole, and inside the magnet their direction is from the south pole to the north pole, so field lines are closed curves.

Second, the relative strength of the field is shown by the degree of closeness of the lines: the field is stronger where the lines are crowded. Third, no two field lines cross each other, because at the point of intersection the compass needle would point towards two directions, which is not possible.

Fourth, the deflection of the needle increases as it is moved along a field line towards the poles.

How to draw one magnetic field line with a compass

Set up

Fix a sheet of white paper on a drawing board, place the bar magnet on it and mark the boundary of the magnet.

First position

Place the compass near the north pole of the magnet. The south pole of the needle points towards the north pole of the magnet, and the north pole of the compass is directed away from the magnet's north pole. Mark the positions of the two ends of the needle.

Step along

Move the needle to a new position such that its south pole occupies the position previously occupied by its north pole.

Continue to the south pole

Proceed step by step in this way until you reach the south pole of the magnet.

Join the marks

Join the points marked on the paper by a smooth curve. This curve represents a field line.

Repeat

Repeat the whole procedure and draw as many lines as you can. Together they represent the magnetic field around the magnet.

Why don't two magnetic field lines intersect each other?

Why?

The direction of the field at a point is the direction in which the north pole of a compass needle would move there. A single point cannot give two directions, so two field lines cannot cross.

Which two methods of producing a magnetic field are shown in this chapter?

Why?

A magnetic field exists in the region surrounding a magnet, and a metallic wire carrying an electric current also has a magnetic field associated with it.

What decides the pattern of the magnetic field generated by a current through a conductor? The pattern depends on the shape of the conductor. Start with a long straight wire.

Take a long straight copper wire, two or three cells of 1.5 V each and a plug key, and connect them in series. Place the straight wire parallel to and over a compass needle, then plug in the key. If the current flows from north to south, the north pole of the compass needle moves towards the east. Now replace the cell connections so that the current flows from south to north; the needle moves towards the west. The direction of the magnetic field is reversed when the current is reversed.

Two more facts come from the same experiment. If you increase the current in the wire, the deflection of the needle increases; so the magnitude of the magnetic field at a given point increases as the current through the wire increases.

If you keep the current the same but move the compass farther from the wire, the deflection decreases; so the field produced by a given current decreases as the distance from the wire increases. The concentric circles representing the field become larger and larger as we move away from the wire.

Which of the following correctly describes the magnetic field near a long straight wire carrying a current?

Why?

Iron filings sprinkled around a straight current-carrying wire arrange themselves in concentric circles around the wire, and these circles represent the field lines.

Here is a convenient way to remember the direction of the field around a straight current-carrying conductor. Imagine that you are holding the conductor in your right hand such that your thumb points towards the direction of the current. Your fingers then wrap around the conductor in the direction of the field lines. This is the right-hand thumb rule.

The same rule is also called Maxwell's corkscrew rule: if you consider yourself driving a corkscrew in the direction of the current, then the direction of rotation of the corkscrew is the direction of the magnetic field.

Worked example

A current through a horizontal power line flows in the east to west direction. What is the direction of the magnetic field at a point directly below it and at a point directly above it?

  1. Point the thumb Hold the power line in your right hand so that your thumb points along the current, that is, from east to west.
  2. Curl the fingers Your fingers wrap around the wire. They give the direction of the field lines in a plane perpendicular to the wire.
  3. Look from one end Viewed from the east end, this curling is clockwise; viewed from the west end it is anti-clockwise.
  4. Same above and below Nothing changes at a point directly below or directly above the wire: the field still turns clockwise in a plane perpendicular to the wire when viewed from the east end, and anti-clockwise when viewed from the west end.

Answer At any point directly below or directly above the wire, the magnetic field turns clockwise in a plane perpendicular to the wire when viewed from the east end, and anti-clockwise when viewed from the west end.

Which rule gives the direction of the magnetic field produced around a straight current-carrying conductor?

Why?

Hold the current-carrying straight conductor in your right hand with the thumb pointing along the current. Your fingers then wrap around the conductor in the direction of the magnetic field lines.

Now bend that straight wire into a circular loop and pass a current through it. The magnetic field of a straight wire depends inversely on the distance from it; in the same way, at every point of a current-carrying circular loop the concentric circles representing the field become larger and larger as we move away from the wire.

By the time we reach the centre of the loop, the arcs of these big circles appear as straight lines. Every point on the wire carrying current gives rise to a magnetic field that appears as a straight line at the centre of the loop. Applying the right-hand rule shows that every section of the wire contributes to the magnetic field lines in the same direction within the loop.

The magnetic field produced by a current-carrying wire at a given point depends directly on the current passing through it. So if a circular coil has n turns, the field produced is n times as large as that produced by a single turn: the current in each circular turn has the same direction, and the fields due to the turns just add up.

A solenoid is a coil with many circular turns wound in the shape of a cylinder. The pattern of the magnetic field lines through and around a current-carrying solenoid is similar to that of a bar magnet, and one end of the solenoid acts as a magnetic north pole. Inside a long straight solenoid carrying current, the magnetic field is the same at all points.

A current-carrying solenoid coil is used to magnetise a steel rod placed inside it. This arrangement is an electromagnet. An electromagnet consists of a core of soft iron wrapped around with a coil of insulated copper wire.

Why?

Inside a long straight solenoid carrying current the field lines are parallel and the field is uniform, so the field is the same at all points inside it.

At the centre of a current-carrying circular coil the arcs of the big circles appear as straight lines, and every section of the wire contributes in the same direction there, so the field lines at the centre are parallel straight lines.

A current-carrying conductor produces a magnetic field, and that field exerts a force on a magnet placed near the conductor. The French scientist Andre Marie Ampere suggested that the magnet must also exert an equal and opposite force on the current-carrying conductor.

Take a small aluminium rod AB of about 5 cm and suspend it horizontally from a stand using two connecting wires. Place a strong horse-shoe magnet so that the rod lies between its two poles with the magnetic field directed upwards: north pole vertically below and south pole vertically above the rod. Connect the rod in series with a battery, a key and a rheostat, and pass a current through the rod from end B to end A. The rod is displaced towards the left. Reverse the direction of the current and the rod is displaced towards the right.

Now change the direction of the field to vertically downwards by interchanging the two poles of the magnet. The direction of the force acting on the current-carrying rod is reversed again. So the direction of the force depends on the direction of the current and on the direction of the magnetic field.

The displacement of the rod is largest, that is, the magnitude of the force is highest, when the direction of the current is at right angles to the direction of the magnetic field. In such a condition a simple rule gives the direction of the force.

Fleming's left-hand rule: stretch the thumb, forefinger and middle finger of your left hand such that they are mutually perpendicular. If the first finger points in the direction of the magnetic field and the second finger in the direction of the current, then the thumb points in the direction of motion or the force acting on the conductor.

Remember that the direction of current is taken opposite to the direction of motion of electrons. Devices that use current-carrying conductors and magnetic fields include the electric motor, the electric generator, loudspeakers, microphones and measuring instruments.

Worked example

An electron moves horizontally towards the east and enters a magnetic field that points vertically upwards, at right angles to its motion. In which direction does the force acting on the electron point?

  1. Fix the field direction The magnetic field points vertically upwards, so the forefinger of the left hand points up.
  2. Find the current direction The electron moves towards the east. The direction of current is taken opposite to the direction of motion of electrons, so the current is towards the west. The second finger points west.
  3. Read the thumb With the forefinger up and the second finger pointing west, the thumb of the left hand points towards the north.
  4. State the result The force acts perpendicular to both the field and the current, so it is directed horizontally towards the north.

Answer The force on the electron acts towards the north.

Electric power reaches a house through a main supply, either supported through overhead electric poles or by underground cables. One wire of this supply, usually with red insulation, is called the live wire (or positive). Another wire, with black insulation, is called the neutral wire (or negative). In our country the potential difference between the two is 220 V.

At the meter-board in the house these wires pass into an electricity meter through a main fuse, and through a main switch they are connected to the line wires in the house. Often two separate circuits are used: one of 15 A current rating for appliances with higher power ratings such as geysers and air coolers, and the other of 5 A current rating for bulbs and fans.

The earth wire has insulation of green colour and is usually connected to a metal plate deep in the earth near the house. It is a safety measure, especially for appliances that have a metallic body, for example an electric press, a toaster, a table fan or a refrigerator. The metallic body is connected to the earth wire, which provides a low-resistance conducting path for the current. Any leakage of current to the metallic body then keeps its potential equal to that of the earth, and the user may not get a severe electric shock.

In each separate circuit, different appliances can be connected across the live and neutral wires, and each appliance has a separate switch to switch the current on and off. In order that each appliance has equal potential difference, the appliances are connected in parallel to each other.

A fuse is an important component of all domestic circuits. It prevents damage to the appliances and the circuit due to overloading. Overloading can occur when the live wire and the neutral wire come into direct contact; this happens when the insulation of wires is damaged or there is a fault in the appliance. In such a situation the current in the circuit abruptly increases, and this is called short-circuiting. The Joule heating that takes place in the fuse melts it to break the electric circuit and stop the flow of an unduly high current.

Overloading can also occur due to an accidental hike in the supply voltage, and sometimes it is caused by connecting too many appliances to a single socket.

An electric current always produces a magnetic field. Even the weak ion currents that travel along the nerve cells in our body produce magnetic fields. When you touch something, your nerves carry an electric impulse to the muscles you need to use, and this impulse produces a temporary magnetic field. Such fields are very weak, about one-billionth of the earth's magnetic field.

The two main organs in the human body where the magnetic field produced is significant are the heart and the brain. The magnetic field inside the body forms the basis of obtaining images of different body parts by a technique called Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), and analysis of these images helps in medical diagnosis.

Glossary

Magnetic field
The region around a magnet in which the force of the magnet can be detected.
Magnetic field line
The line along which iron filings line up around a magnet; a compass needle placed on it points along its direction.
Right-hand thumb rule
A rule for the direction of the field around a straight current-carrying wire: thumb along the current, curled fingers along the field.
Fleming's left-hand rule
A rule for the direction of the force on a current-carrying conductor: forefinger along the field, second finger along the current, thumb along the force.
Solenoid
A coil of many circular turns wound in the shape of a cylinder, whose field is like that of a bar magnet.
Electromagnet
A soft iron core wrapped with a coil of insulated copper wire, made magnetic by passing a current through the coil.
Electric fuse
A safety device that melts and breaks the circuit when too large a current flows, protecting appliances from damage.
Short-circuiting
A fault in which the live wire and the neutral wire touch directly, so the current in the circuit abruptly increases.
Earth wire
The green-insulated safety wire connected to a metal plate in the earth, which keeps a metallic appliance body at the earth's potential.

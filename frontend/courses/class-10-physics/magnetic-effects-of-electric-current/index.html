Magnetic Effects of Electric Current

After this lesson you can:

  • After this lesson you can describe the magnetic field of a magnet and its field lines, and list the properties of field lines.
  • After this lesson you can find the direction of the magnetic field produced by a current in a straight wire, in a circular loop and in a solenoid, using the right-hand thumb rule.
  • After this lesson you can use Fleming's left-hand rule to find the direction of the force on a current-carrying conductor placed in a magnetic field.
  • After this lesson you can describe the wires, the safety devices and the common faults of a domestic electric circuit.

A current in a wire can heat the wire, but it can do something else too. A wire carrying an electric current behaves like a magnet.

Connect a straight thick copper wire between two points X and Y of a circuit, keeping the wire perpendicular to the plane of the paper. Place a small compass horizontally near the wire, note the position of its needle, then insert the key and pass a current. The needle is deflected.

The deflection means that the electric current through the copper wire has produced a magnetic effect. So electricity and magnetism are linked to each other.

Hans Christian Oersted, one of the leading scientists of the 19th century, found this in 1820. He accidentally discovered that a compass needle got deflected when an electric current passed through a metallic wire placed nearby, and through this observation he showed that electricity and magnetism were related phenomena. The unit of magnetic field strength is named the oersted in his honour.

A compass needle placed near a straight copper wire is deflected when the key is inserted and current flows. What does this show?

Why?

The needle is deflected only while the current flows, so the current itself is producing a magnetic field around the wire. That is why electricity and magnetism are said to be linked.

A compass needle gets deflected when brought near a bar magnet. A compass needle is, in fact, a small bar magnet: its ends point approximately towards north and south. The end pointing towards north is called the north-seeking or north pole, and the other end, pointing towards south, is the south-seeking or south pole.

Fix a sheet of white paper on a drawing board and place a bar magnet at the centre. Sprinkle iron filings uniformly around the magnet, using a salt-sprinkler if you like, and tap the board gently. The filings arrange themselves in a pattern, because the magnet exerts its influence in the region surrounding it and the filings experience a force. The region surrounding a magnet, in which the force of the magnet can be detected, is said to have a magnetic field. The lines along which the iron filings align themselves represent magnetic field lines.

You can draw the field lines yourself with a small compass and a bar magnet. Place the magnet on a sheet of paper fixed on a drawing board and mark the boundary of the magnet. Place the compass near the north pole: the south pole of the needle points towards the north pole of the magnet, and the north pole of the compass is directed away from it.

Mark the position of the two ends of the needle. Now move the compass to a new position such that its south pole occupies the position previously occupied by its north pole. Proceed step by step till you reach the south pole of the magnet. Join the points marked on the paper by a smooth curve: this curve represents a field line. Repeat the procedure and draw as many lines as you can; the pattern you get is the magnetic field around the magnet. As you move the needle along a field line towards the poles, the deflection increases.

Magnetic field is a quantity that has both direction and magnitude. The direction of the magnetic field at a point is taken to be the direction in which a north pole of the compass needle moves inside it. So by convention, field lines emerge from the north pole and merge at the south pole. Inside the magnet the direction of field lines is from its south pole to its north pole. Thus magnetic field lines are closed curves.

The relative strength of the magnetic field is shown by the degree of closeness of the field lines. The field is stronger, that is, the force acting on the pole of another magnet placed there is greater, where the field lines are crowded.

No two field lines cross each other. If they did, it would mean that at the point of intersection the compass needle would point towards two directions, which is not possible.

Weak magnetic fields are present in our bodies too. Ion currents along nerve cells produce temporary fields about one-billionth of the earth's magnetic field. The heart and the brain produce significant fields, and the magnetic field inside the body is the basis of obtaining images of body parts by a technique called Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI).

Why does a compass needle get deflected when brought near a bar magnet?

Why?

A compass needle is itself a small bar magnet. Inside the magnetic field of the bar magnet it experiences a force, so it turns and its ends point along the field lines.

No two field lines are found to cross each other. If they did, then at the point of intersection the compass needle would point towards two directions at the same time, which is not possible.

Pass a current through a long straight thick copper wire held vertically, and sprinkle iron filings uniformly on a cardboard through which the wire passes. Tapping the cardboard gently makes the filings arrange themselves in a pattern of concentric circles around the copper wire. These concentric circles represent the magnetic field lines.

Place a compass at a point on one circle. The direction of the north pole of the compass needle gives the direction of the field line at that point, and you can show this direction by an arrow. If the direction of the current through the straight wire is reversed, the direction of the magnetic field lines is also reversed.

Field of a straight wire: change the current, change the distance

A straight wire is seen end on, with a compass placed at a chosen distance from it. The concentric field circles are drawn around the wire. Increase the current and the needle turns further; move the compass farther away and the turn becomes smaller. The turn of the needle is shown in words only (small, medium, large), because the chapter tells us only that the deflection increases with current and decreases with distance, not by how much.

What happens to the deflection of a compass needle placed at a given point if the current in the copper wire is changed? Vary the current and watch. If the current is increased, the deflection also increases. This indicates that the magnitude of the magnetic field produced at a given point increases as the current through the wire increases.

What happens if the compass is moved away from the copper wire while the current stays the same? Place the compass at a farther point. The deflection in the needle decreases. Thus the magnetic field produced by a given current in the conductor decreases as the distance from it increases, and the concentric circles representing the field around a current-carrying straight wire become larger and larger as we move away from it.

Here is a convenient way of finding the direction of the magnetic field associated with a current-carrying conductor. Imagine that you are holding a current-carrying straight conductor in your right hand such that the thumb points towards the direction of the current. Then your fingers will wrap around the conductor in the direction of the field lines of the magnetic field. This is known as the right-hand thumb rule.

This rule is also called Maxwell's corkscrew rule. If we consider ourselves driving a corkscrew in the direction of the current, then the direction of the rotation of the corkscrew is the direction of the magnetic field.

Use it like this: first fix the direction of the current with your thumb, then read the direction of the field from the way your fingers curl. The field circles lie in planes perpendicular to the wire.

Worked example

A current through a horizontal power line flows in east to west direction. What is the direction of magnetic field at a point directly below it and at a point directly above it?

  1. Note the direction of the current The power line is horizontal and the current flows from east to west.
  2. Apply the right-hand thumb rule Hold the wire in your right hand with the thumb pointing along the current, from east to west. Your fingers then curl around the wire in the direction of the magnetic field.
  3. Picture the field circles The field lines are circles lying in a plane perpendicular to the wire, that is, in a vertical plane running north-south. The field at a point directly below the wire and at a point directly above the wire both lie on these circles.
  4. Read off the sense of turning Looking along the wire from the east end, the curling of the fingers — and so the field — turns clockwise; looking from the west end, the same field turns anti-clockwise.

Answer The magnetic field at any point below or above the wire turns clockwise in a plane perpendicular to the wire when viewed from the east end, and anti-clockwise when viewed from the west end.

Suppose the straight wire is bent in the form of a circular loop and a current is passed through it. The magnetic field produced by a current-carrying straight wire depends inversely on the distance from it, so at every point of the loop the circles representing the field become larger and larger as we move away from the wire.

By the time we reach the centre of the circular loop, the arcs of these big circles appear as straight lines. Every point on the wire carrying current gives rise to a magnetic field that appears as a straight line at the centre of the loop. Applying the right-hand rule, it is easy to check that every section of the wire contributes to the magnetic field lines in the same direction within the loop.

Why?

Apply the right-hand rule to the loop: curl your fingers in the direction of the current, which is clockwise seen from above, and the thumb points away from you — downwards into the table. So the field inside the loop is perpendicular to the table, pointing downwards, and outside the loop it points the other way.

The magnetic field produced by a current-carrying wire at a given point depends directly on the current passing through it. Therefore, if there is a circular coil having n turns, the field produced is n times as large as that produced by a single turn. This is because the current in each circular turn has the same direction, and the field due to each turn then just adds up.

A solenoid is a coil of many circular turns of insulated copper wire wound in the shape of a cylinder. Its magnetic field lines are like those of a bar magnet: one end of the solenoid acts as a magnetic north pole. The field lines inside the solenoid are parallel straight lines, and the magnetic field is uniform — the same at all points — inside a long solenoid carrying current.

A strong magnetic field inside a current-carrying solenoid is used to magnetise a piece of steel placed inside it. An electromagnet consists of a core of soft iron wrapped around with a coil of insulated copper wire.

An electric current flowing through a conductor produces a magnetic field, and the field so produced exerts a force on a magnet placed in the vicinity of the conductor. The French scientist Andre Marie Ampere suggested that the magnet must also exert an equal and opposite force on the current-carrying conductor.

Suspend a small aluminium rod AB, about 5 cm long, horizontally from a stand using two connecting wires. Place a strong horse-shoe magnet so that the rod lies between the two poles with the magnetic field directed upwards: the north pole vertically below and the south pole vertically above the rod. Connect the rod in series with a battery, a key and a rheostat, and pass a current through the rod from end B to end A. The rod is displaced. Reverse the direction of the current and the rod is displaced in the opposite direction.

Now interchange the two poles of the magnet so that the field is vertically downwards. Once again the direction of the force acting on the current-carrying rod gets reversed. This shows that the direction of the force on the conductor depends upon the direction of the current and the direction of the magnetic field.

Experiments have shown that the displacement of the rod is largest, that is, the magnitude of the force is the highest, when the direction of the current is at right angles to the direction of the magnetic field. In such a condition we can use a simple rule to find the direction of the force on the conductor.

The rule is Fleming's left-hand rule. Stretch the thumb, forefinger and middle finger of your left hand such that they are mutually perpendicular. If the first finger points in the direction of the magnetic field and the second finger in the direction of the current, then the thumb will point in the direction of motion, or the force acting on the conductor.

Devices that use current-carrying conductors and magnetic fields include the electric motor, the electric generator, loudspeakers, microphones and measuring instruments.

Worked example

An electron enters a magnetic field at right angles to it. The direction of force acting on the electron will be (a) to the right. (b) to the left. (c) out of the page. (d) into the page.

  1. Fix the direction of the current The direction of current is taken opposite to the direction of motion of electrons. So first turn the electron's direction of motion round by half a turn to get the direction of the current.
  2. Note the angle between current and field The electron enters at right angles to the field, so the current is also at right angles to the field — this is the case where the force is largest and Fleming's left-hand rule applies directly.
  3. Apply Fleming's left-hand rule Point the first finger along the magnetic field and the second finger along the current (opposite to the electron's motion). The thumb then gives the direction of the force, which is perpendicular to both the field and the current.
  4. Read the answer from the figure Carrying this out for the directions shown gives a force directed into the page.

Answer Option (d): the force is directed into the page.

In our homes we receive supply of electric power through a main supply, either supported through overhead electric poles or by underground cables. One of the wires in this supply, usually with red insulation cover, is called the live wire, or positive wire. Another wire, with black insulation, is called the neutral wire, or negative wire. In our country the potential difference between the two is 220 V, and we receive AC electric power of 220 V with a frequency of 50 Hz.

At the meter-board in the house, these wires pass into an electricity meter through a main fuse. Through the main switch they are connected to the line wires in the house. These wires supply electricity to separate circuits within the house. Often two separate circuits are used: one of 15 A current rating for appliances with higher power ratings such as geysers and air coolers, and the other of 5 A current rating for bulbs, fans, etc.

The earth wire, which has insulation of green colour, is usually connected to a metal plate deep in the earth near the house. This is used as a safety measure, especially for those appliances that have a metallic body, for example an electric press, toaster, table fan or refrigerator. The metallic body is connected to the earth wire, which provides a low-resistance conducting path for the current. Thus it ensures that any leakage of current to the metallic body of the appliance keeps its potential to that of the earth, and the user may not get a severe electric shock.

In each separate circuit, different appliances can be connected across the live and neutral wires, and each appliance has a separate switch to switch the current through it on and off. In order that each appliance has equal potential difference, they are connected in parallel to each other.

An electric fuse is an important component of all domestic circuits. A fuse in a circuit prevents damage to the appliances and the circuit due to overloading. Overloading can occur when the live wire and the neutral wire come into direct contact. This happens when the insulation of wires is damaged or there is a fault in the appliance. In such a situation the current in the circuit abruptly increases. This is called short-circuiting.

The use of an electric fuse prevents the electric circuit and the appliance from a possible damage by stopping the flow of unduly high electric current. The Joule heating that takes place in the fuse melts it and breaks the electric circuit. Overloading can also occur due to an accidental hike in the supply voltage, and sometimes by connecting too many appliances to a single socket.

Glossary

magnetic field
The region around a magnet in which the force of the magnet can be detected; it has both direction and magnitude.
magnetic field line
A smooth curve drawn with the help of iron filings or a compass needle showing the direction of the magnetic field; lines emerge from the north pole, merge at the south pole and never cross.
right-hand thumb rule
Point the thumb of your right hand along the current in a straight conductor; your curled fingers then show the direction of the magnetic field lines.
solenoid
A coil of many circular turns of insulated copper wire wound in the shape of a cylinder; its field is like that of a bar magnet and is uniform inside.
electromagnet
A soft iron core wrapped with a coil of insulated copper wire, which becomes a strong magnet when current flows through the coil.
Fleming's left-hand rule
First finger along the magnetic field, second finger along the current, thumb along the force on the conductor.
live wire
The domestic supply wire with red insulation, also called the positive wire.
neutral wire
The domestic supply wire with black insulation, also called the negative wire; the potential difference between live and neutral is 220 V.
earth wire
The green-insulated wire connected to a metal plate deep in the earth, giving a low-resistance path so that a leakage current does not give a severe shock.
short-circuiting
Direct contact between the live and neutral wires (damaged insulation or a faulty appliance) that makes the current abruptly increase.
overloading
A condition where the current in a circuit becomes too large, from short-circuiting, a hike in supply voltage, or too many appliances on one socket.
fuse
A safety device that melts by Joule heating and breaks the circuit when the current is too large.
